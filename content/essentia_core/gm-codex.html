Game Master Codex: Formulas and Mechanics

This codex contains formulas, probability tables, and mechanical calculations for Game Masters. References to this codex appear throughout the rules; here you will find the mathematical framework and scenario modifiers needed to run your game.

Witness Probability Formula

The Witness Probability determines if a character's secret deed is discovered. The GM rolls privately and tracks cumulative probability. Success means the deed remains hidden; failure means witnesses exist and the GM may later reveal them or issue Shamed Titles.

Baseline Probabilities by Scenario

ScenarioBaseline ProbabilityDescription
Desolate Area, Night, No Creatures5%A remote, isolated location with no magical creatures, wisps, or observers. Low light, late hour. Examples: abandoned ruins at midnight, empty forest clearing after dark.
Desolate Area, Day, Magical Creatures Present15%Remote location but wisps, spirits, or other magical entities may be watching. Daylight makes the act more visible if observers are present. Examples: magical forest during day, ancient altar with spectral guardians.
Sparse Area (1-5 NPCs nearby)25%A quiet area with a few people or creatures within potential earshot or sight. Examples: small camp, quiet tavern, lightly traveled road.
Populated Area (6-20 NPCs nearby)50%A moderately busy location where the deed could easily be seen or heard. Examples: crowded tavern, busy marketplace, guardhouse.
Highly Populated Area (20+ NPCs nearby)75%A very crowded or bustling location where secrecy is difficult. Examples: festival, main street, throne room with court.

Modifiers

Apply these modifiers to the baseline probability:

  • Time of Day: Night -10%, Twilight -5%, Day +0%, Noon (bright sun) +10%
  • Character Actions: Leaving obvious evidence +15%, Fleeing conspicuously +10%, Acting naturally -10%, Disguising self -15%
  • Deed Severity: Minor (theft, minor deception) +0%, Moderate (assault, property damage) +10%, Severe (murder, betrayal) +20%
  • Magical Creatures/Wisps: Per creature present +5% (stacks; wisps are supernatural observers)
  • Character Reputation: Known criminal +10%, Known hero -10%, Unknown +0%

Cumulative Probability: Repeat Offenses

Each time a character commits the same deed type (or similar deeds) in the same location, the cumulative probability increases:

New Probability = Base Probability + (Occurrences × 10%) Example: First murder in a forest: 5% + 0 = 5% Second murder in same forest: 5% + 10% = 15% Third murder in same forest: 5% + 20% = 25% Fourth murder in same forest: 5% + 30% = 35%

The multiplication represents increased magical, spiritual, or natural awareness in that location. Wisps remember. Spirits linger. NPCs share stories. The world grows smaller.

Cap: Cumulative probability caps at 95%. Even a skilled criminal may avoid absolute certainty, but persistence guarantees eventual discovery.

Game Master Process: Rolling Witness Probability

  1. Determine the scenario and baseline probability from the table above.
  2. Apply modifiers based on time, character actions, deed severity, creatures, and reputation.
  3. Roll 1d100 secretly.
  4. If the roll meets or exceeds the adjusted probability, the deed was NOT witnessed. Keep this result secret.
  5. If the roll is below the probability, the deed WAS witnessed. Record this privately and reveal at an opportune story moment.
  6. Increase cumulative probability if this deed repeats in the same location.

Revelation and Consequences

A discovered deed does not immediately result in consequences. The GM may:

  • Hold the information and reveal it later when an NPC or faction brings it up.
  • Allow the witness to demand payment, favor, or silence.
  • Let the witness spread rumors or inform factions.
  • Issue a Shamed Title reflecting the deed (see Titles, Appendix).

The GM controls when and how the deed is exposed. A character who commits murder in secret may never face consequences if no witness was rolled, or they may face them years later when an old witness reappears. This uncertainty is intentional.

Professions: Social Roles & Expertise

Professions represent specialized roles and expertise available in the game world. The framework distinguishes between Character Professions (player-accessible roles paired with Talent Trees) and NPC Professions (all available social roles for non-players). GMs use these systems to populate the world with believable specialists, craftspeople, officials, and service providers.

Character Professions

Structure: Thirty Character Professions organized into four categories: Crafting (9), Building (7), Automation (9), and Exploration (2). Each pairs with a specific Talent Tree to create synergy; combined advancement of both yields mechanical benefits beyond either alone.

GM Application: When player characters pursue a profession, allow them access to NPCs who can teach that profession (Profession Trainers) and to other NPCs who depend on or work with that profession (see Disposition Logic in each entry). Profession advancement is mechanical: a character's Proficiency and Talents unlock higher-difficulty Professional Skills.

Reference: Character Professions

Non-Player Character Professions

Structure: One hundred-one NPC Professions organized into thirteen categories spanning Commerce (12), Construction (7), Adventurer (11), Justice (15), Technology (12), Mysticism (11), Transit (8), Interactive (10), Restoration (4), Terraforming (3), Production (12), Administration (4), and Husbandry (2). NPCs pursue professions independent of Talent Trees, allowing flexible role assignment.

GM Application: Use NPC Professions to quickly populate settlements, organizations, and factions with believable specialists. An NPC's profession defines their social function, authority, and likely interactions with player characters. A Tax Collector NPC has legal standing to extract coin; an Auctioneer has access to wealthy patrons; a Bounty Hunter may pursue player characters if hired to do so.

Overlapping Professions: Some professions (marked with *) appear in both Character and Non-Player Character lists: Cooks, Architects, Artificers, Scientists, and others. When a player character enters these professions, they compete or collaborate with NPC professionals in that field. A player Blacksmith may apprentice under an NPC Master Blacksmith or rival a famous NPC forgemaster.

Reference: NPC Professions

Profession Disposition Networks

What It Is: Each profession entry (especially Character Professions) includes a "Disposition Logic" section explaining how that profession interacts with 1-3 NPC Dispositions. These are the professional relationships, patrons, and allied specialists that make professions mechanically and narratively functional.

GM Application: When a player character pursues a profession, introduce the associated NPC Dispositions early. An Alchemist synergizes with Chemists, Inventors, and vendors; introduce these NPCs as natural allies or competitors. A Cook works with Innkeepers and Vendors; let the player develop relationships with these NPCs through the profession's progression. Disposition networks create organic quest hooks and social bonds.

Profession Vs. Class

Professions are not the same as Classes (Clerics, Warriors, Artificers, etc.). A character's Class determines their combat capabilities and spell-casting abilities. A character's Profession determines their non-combat expertise, social role, and economic function in the world. A Cleric (Class) may be an Apothecary (Profession). A Warrior may be a Blacksmith. A Magic User may be a Scientist. Professions cross class boundaries and can be mixed freely; mechanically, a character advances one profession at a time, but narratively, many real-world people wear multiple professional hats.
