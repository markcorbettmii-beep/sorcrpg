Time

Essentia measures its years in Eons of Adoria (EA), the current year is 10,023 EA. Below that, the calendar runs on a simpler, table-friendly structure.

The Calendar

1 Eon of Adoria (1 EA) is 360 days, divided into 12 months of 30 days each. Turns and Rounds (see Turns, Rounds and Time, pg. 5) measure combat within a single day; the calendar measures everything beyond it, travel, downtime, seasons, and a character's life itself. This calendar is Zailister's own, every world of Essentia keeps its own seasons, and a planet's climate and light cycle (see each world's entry under Planetary Markets and Into the Uncharted Worlds of Essentia) do not necessarily follow Zailister's 4-season, 12-month pattern at all.

Zailister divides its year into 4 seasons of 3 months each, and marks the turning point of each with a festival, 8 in total spaced 45 days apart: Emberturn opens the year at the winter solstice, followed by Firstlight, Bloomrise (spring equinox), Sunwake, Highsun (summer solstice), Goldfall, Duskbalance (autumn equinox), and The Umbral Vigil, which closes the cycle back toward Emberturn (see Festivals & Seasonal Events, Zail pg. 1). These festivals are cultural observances, not the basis of the calendar itself, the day/month/year count above holds regardless of what is or isn't being celebrated.

Mortality, Immortality, and Retirement

Time is what eventually catches up with every character. Mortal races age against their natural lifespan (see each race's Lifespan tag); Immortal races don't, but every character still retires. Starting Age scales with a race's natural maturity, a quarter of their natural lifespan, or an effective 400 years for Immortal races, while the adventuring window itself stays the same 40 years for every race, so Retirement Age is simply Starting Age plus 40 (see each race's entry, pg. 2, and Retirement under Leveling and Advancement, pg. 2). Players choose their character's actual Starting Age from a short range at creation rather than a single fixed number, see Age under Character Creation, pg. 2. A character's Rank at that moment decides what's left behind: reach Legend and the world remembers the Character in monuments, statues, and songs; retire below it and the recognition is quieter. See Legacy under Prestige, pg. 2, for how this same legend-building plays out in Prestige while a character is still active.

Aura: Presence and Life Stage

Aura is a Character's physical presence. It is what other people sense of them in a room, before a word is spoken and before a name is given. Aura stands apart from Prestige and is not fed by it. It reads through five channels:

  • Sight: how they look and how they carry it. Bearing, condition, the state of what they wear, and whatever the eye lands on first.
  • Smell: what they carry on them. Incense, blood, smoke, rot, clean linen, sweat, the road.
  • Sound: voice, breath, and the noise they make simply existing. Armor that rattles, a joint that clicks, a step that makes no sound at all.
  • Touch: what contact with them is like. Cold, feverish, calloused, dry, slick, slimy.
  • Gut: the one with no organ behind it. What people feel standing near them and cannot account for, the reason a room quiets or a dog will not settle.

A Character cannot have multiple Auras; they have one Aura that evolves as they age and their life circumstances change. Aura can move a Character's Reputation on its own. A man who smells of rot and draws flies is spoken of that way wherever he goes, and a pauper who carries himself like a king is spoken of that way too.

Movement

Movement is calculated by how fast any moving thing can move across squares on a grid, in units of feet, per its turn. The size of each square is determined by the grid type, for instance, a battle map uses 5 ft. squares. Other map and grid types such as war maps, traveling paths, wilderness and others are covered later in this section.

Base speed is determined by race size, Goliath 40 ft. (8 squares), Standard 30 ft. (6 squares), and Small 25 ft. (5 squares). Individual races may vary within their size category, refer to each race's entry for specifics.

All maps use real planetary isometric coordinates, scaling seamlessly from world maps down to the square a character is standing on.

Terrain affects how far a character can move per action. Conditions such as rough ground, strong winds, slippery surfaces, or currents can reduce movement, while favorable conditions, a downhill slope, wind at one's back, or a strong current, can extend it. Some terrain types also require attribute or talent checks to navigate successfully.

Not Everywhere Is a Shortcut Away: Teleport, Warp, FTL/Light Speed, and the Wayfarer all exist and all get characters somewhere fast, but every one of them runs on a cooldown, a preparation cost, or a beacon that has to be set first. None of them make the wider world optional. A floating island with no marked landing point, a valley walled off by cliffs, a station reachable only by ship, these still have to be reached on land, by sea, or by air, the slow way, and plenty of them stay that way permanently, even long after they've been found and mapped.

Movement Type Rules

Prone: A character who is prone must spend half an action to stand up. While prone, melee attacks against them gain a bonus and ranged attacks are penalized. Crawling while prone costs 1/4 speed. Prone from unconsciousness, incapacitation, or death is covered in Combat and Conditions.

Fly: Flying characters must have a fly speed from an ability, spell, or mount. A flying character who is incapacitated, stunned, or loses their fly source falls. Ascending costs double movement, descending is free but may require a check at steep angles. Hovering requires no movement but requires an ability or innate trait to maintain.

Glide: Controlled descent only, a gliding character cannot gain altitude and moves 1x horizontally for every square dropped. Stalling or incapacitation results in a fall. Requires an innate ability, wings, or equipment.

Swim: Swimming characters must have a swim speed or make checks to stay afloat. A character can hold their breath for a number of rounds equal to their Strength score. At zero breath, they begin drowning, taking Life damage each round until they surface or are rescued.

Wading: Wading through water, brush, or heavy foliage reduces movement to 1/2x. Severe conditions may require a Strength or Agility Trait check specified by module or determined by the GM.

Climb / Scale: A climbing character who fails their check stops moving that turn. A critical fail results in a fall, damage based on height specified by module or determined by the GM. Heavy loads increase check difficulty. Swinging on a rope or vine follows the same rules, a failed check results in a slip or drop.

Rappel: Requires rope or equivalent equipment and a talent check. A failed check results in a slip of a number of squares specified by module or determined by the GM. A critical fail results in a full fall.

Burrow / Tunnel: A burrowing character is hidden from above-ground sight and cannot be targeted by ranged attacks unless the attacker has a sense or ability that detects underground movement. Surfacing costs half movement. Underground visibility is zero without appropriate senses or equipment.

Crawl: If not prone, crawling can be used deliberately for stealth at 1/4 speed.

Slither / Squeeze: Slither is limbless movement available only to creatures with the Slither Ability, they cannot stand up and may move through gaps smaller than their standard size. Any entity may squeeze through a space smaller than their size category at the same 1/4x speed. Both Agility and Dexterity Trait checks are required to squeeze, both Traits stack. A failed check stops movement for that turn, and a critical fail may result in becoming stuck at GM discretion. Squeezing through a hostile creature's space is not permitted unless that creature is incapacitated. Small and Tiny creatures rarely need to squeeze, as most spaces accommodate their size naturally. A tight space is any opening narrower than the space a size category normally occupies. A Small or Standard entity squeezes through anything under 5 ft. wide, a Large entity through anything under 10 ft., a Goliath through anything under 20 ft. See Size Categories table.

Zero Gravity: Without training or equipment, movement becomes uncontrolled and requires a check each turn to navigate. With training or equipment, movement is 1x in any direction including vertical. Pushing off a surface grants a momentum bonus.

Magnetic (metal surface): Requires magnetic boots or equivalent equipment. Movement is 1/2x on any metal surface including walls and ceilings. Cannot be knocked prone while active.

Phase: Available only to characters with an innate phasing ability. Cannot attack or be attacked while phasing. Cannot end movement inside a solid object, doing so results in immediate incapacitation.

Run / Dash: Standard 2x speed movement. Skipping, bounding, and similar movement styles are flavor variants of Run and follow the same rules.

Moving Around Other Creatures: Moving through a friendly creature's space is permitted but their space is considered difficult terrain. Moving through a hostile creature's space is not permitted unless the creature is incapacitated. Moving past a hostile creature within reach may trigger an ATTK of Opportunity. Squeezing through a space smaller than a size category costs double movement and requires an Agility and Dexterity Trait check, both Traits stack, and a failed check stops movement. More on hostile creature interactions in the Combat section.

Falling: A falling character takes damage based on the distance fallen. Traits such as Dexterity and Defense stack to reduce falling damage. Organic mount, ground: Rider uses the mount's base speed. Mounting and dismounting costs half an action. If the mount is incapacitated, the rider must make an Agility Trait check to dismount safely or fall.

Non-organic vehicle, ground (steam, coal, gas, electric, photon, quantum, or magic-powered): Follows the driver's control checks for turns, stops, and hazards. Damage to the vehicle reduces speed, specified by module or determined by the GM. Magic-powered vehicles and constructs may also have their movement manipulated directly by Nature or Spell Abilities, the same as organic movement.

Non-organic vehicle, hover: Follows hover rules. Can maintain a stationary air position. Damage to the vehicle reduces speed or causes descent, specified by module or determined by the GM.

Teleport: Requires line of sight or a marked beacon. Cannot teleport to an unseen or unmarked location. Failed attempts result in the Character staying in place. Teleporting into an occupied space is not permitted.

Blink: Short range only, maximum range determined by the ability or item. Does not require line of sight but requires a clear destination. Can be used as a reaction to avoid damage once per round at GM discretion.

Warp: Long range teleportation requiring significant preparation or power. Warping to an unmarked location carries risk, a failed check results in arriving off target, specified by module or determined by the GM.

Spacecraft: Follows non-organic vehicle rules. Sub-light speed applies in atmosphere. Space grid scale applies in open space. Light speed capable spacecraft may achieve FTL travel, see FTL below. Boarding and deboarding require docking or a check if in motion.

FTL / Light speed: Available to Divine entities with Blink or equivalent innate magic, and to light speed capable spacecraft. Cannot be used in atmosphere or near planetary bodies without catastrophic consequences specified by module or determined by the GM. No check required for Divine entities, it is innate. Spacecraft require appropriate technology and fuel.

Quicker Feet: The ability to change direction at every 5 ft. square during movement without penalty. Not just speed, unpredictability. Characters and creatures with Quicker Feet can reverse, cut, weave, and redirect mid-stride in ways that make tracking them a genuine puzzle in the field. Applies to all movement types available to them.

Note to GM: Quicker Feet means changing direction 90 degrees every 5 ft. square (zigzagging). Each pair of moves gives diagonal net progress: 2 squares moved = √2 squares net (≈ 7.07 ft. net per 10 ft. moved). To achieve 35 ft. of net displacement: 35 ÷ (√2/2) = 35 × √2 ≈ 49.5 ft., rounded to 50 ft. A creature with Quicker Feet and 25 ft. speed covers roughly 17.5 ft. of net displacement per turn when zigzagging at full movement.

Pulse Jet Propulsion: Reserved for creatures with the innate Pulse Jet Ability. A sharp contraction fires water backward and sends the creature rocketing forward, up to 25 ft. per burst. The catch is physics: water drag kills momentum the instant the pulse ends. The creature must change direction at every 5 ft. square, a straight line simply isn't possible, and that's exactly what makes it dangerous. Defenders trying to intercept a Pulse Jet creature have to guess where it's going, not where it came from. Cannot be used outside of water unless the creature's card specifically states otherwise.

Thruster Burst: Requires a TABA thruster modification. PTS, fire, and then let the environment decide how complicated things get. Direction is committed at the start of movement. What happens next depends entirely on the surrounding environment.

In vacuum: No drag. No forgiveness. Momentum carries into the following turn, a character who ends their turn moving begins the next from that same vector. Changing direction mid-move requires a counter-thrust: spend half the remaining movement to cancel momentum before redirecting. Changing direction at the start of a new turn costs the same. Fastest for sustained travel. Most dangerous to get wrong.

In atmosphere with gravity, horizontal: Air drag and gravity handle the counter-thrust automatically. Momentum resets at the end of each turn. Direction is still committed at the start of a move but changes freely at the beginning of each new turn. Mid-move direction changes cost half remaining movement. Closest to a standard Run in feel, with considerably more noise.

In atmosphere with gravity, ascending: Fighting gravity the whole way up. Speed drops to 2x. Angling the thrusters laterally while ascending drops speed to 1x. Cannot redirect mid-ascent without paying that cost. Sustained fuel required each round of ascent.

In atmosphere with gravity, descending: Choose one mode at the start of the move and commit. Braking: 1x speed downward; active before impact negates fall damage entirely. Hovering: no movement; stationary in the air; sustained fuel cost each round, run out and gravity decides the rest. Assisted Descent: 4x speed downward; direction committed; no braking that same turn.

Underwater: Drag is severe and immediate. Speed drops to 1x. Direction changes freely at every 5 ft. sq., the water handles counter-thrust naturally. Functions like a slower, more controlled version of Pulse Jet Propulsion: deliberate where Pulse Jet is instinctive.

Animated Wayfarer

The Animated Wayfarer, or simply the Wayfarer, is a living magical companion bound to its owner at attunement. It is not a passive tool. It moves, reacts, and grows alongside the Character, developing awareness, loyalty, and capability over time. The Wayfarer bonds to the Character's heart, it knows whether they truly feel safe, and it will not take them somewhere they don't. It appears and disappears at will, never leaving the traveler's side for long.

Wayfarers come in eleven forms, each animated and each with its own default behavior and unique higher-LVL abilities. The form is cosmetic at base, all Wayfarers share the same core abilities as they LVL, but each form develops its own specialization as it grows.

Forms: Pocket Watch, Compass, Sextant, Astrolabe, Hourglass, Spyglass, Monocle, Lantern, Signet Ring, Orrery, Runestone Disc.

Cooldown

The Wayfarer's port ability begins at once per week. Leveling reduces this cooldown, the more developed the Wayfarer, the more frequently it can be used. Wayfarer development is exceptionally slow. Progress is earned, not given.

Complex Rule Set only.

Base Ability (Level 0)

Port: The Wayfarer ports its owner to the nearest source of fire or water, whichever is closest. This is the foundation of all Wayfarer use and functions at all LVLs.

Generic Abilities, Unlocked Through Progression

All Wayfarers unlock the following abilities as they LVL, regardless of form. Abilities unlock during exploration as the Wayfarer develops.

  • Nearest Camp: Port to the nearest camp rather than fire or water.
  • Faction Sense: Port to the nearest town or city with a qualifying Class Trainer, rather than fire or water. At higher LVLs, it senses its owner's standing with that settlement's factions in advance, alerting whether they have sufficient Standing to be trained there, and steering toward, or away from, a particular town within its range (see Class Trainer Requirements & Fees, Appendix).
  • Mark Spot: Mark a location and return to it on command, a plot of land a character owns, a hideout, a cache site, anywhere worth remembering and revisiting. Log marked spots in the Journal's Maps, Locations & Wayfarer Log.
  • Alert: The Wayfarer senses danger before it arrives, it stirs, pulses, or otherwise signals that something is wrong. At higher LVLs, it identifies the direction and nature of the threat.
  • Find Secret: The Wayfarer motions toward hidden locations, secret passages, fishing spots, buried caches, and forgotten areas, the quiet places a character finds and comes to think of as their own. Tied to exploration achievements. At a certain LVL it actively leads rather than hints.
  • Deep Down: The Wayfarer can guide its owner deep into dungeons, castles, estates, and prisons, including rooms buried far enough down, throne rooms, vaults, and sealed chambers among them, that they require a light source physically present at the destination for the Wayfarer to find its bearing. Places the Wayfarer would otherwise lose its way entirely.
  • All Aboard: The Wayfarer can locate and guide its owner into the hold of nearby vessels, both seafaring ships and spacecraft.
  • Quest Guidance: The Wayfarer motions and cues its owner toward things of importance, active quests, quest items, and new quest NPCs. At low LVLs it nudges. At higher LVLs it leads confidently.

Form Special Abilities

Each form develops five special abilities unique to it, unlocking progressively as the Wayfarer LVLs.

Pocket Watch, Tracks time as its default. Specializes in cooldown reduction and temporal awareness.

  1. Shows accurate local time across regions and planets.
  2. Cooldown reduced by one day.
  3. Predicts weather up to 24 hours ahead.
  4. Cooldown reduced to once per day.
  5. Skips one danger event per session, the Watch turns back just enough.

Compass, Shows cardinal directions at base. Specializes in navigation and ally awareness.

  1. Detects the direction of the nearest beacon.
  2. Highlights the safest path through hostile terrain.
  3. PTS toward the nearest ally when the party is separated.
  4. Navigation bonus increases significantly, the owner cannot become lost.
  5. PTS toward the nearest undiscovered location in the region.

Sextant, Measures celestial angles at base. Excels outdoors, at sea, and in space.

  1. Identifies the owner's exact position on any map.
  2. Alert range extended under open sky or in vacuum.
  3. Reads star patterns to anticipate enemy movements.
  4. Locks onto orbiting vessels or space stations within range.
  5. Pinpoints the exact coordinates of any previously marked location.

Astrolabe, Maps star positions at base. Strongest at revealing secrets and ancient sites.

  1. Reveals hidden celestial landmarks and navigational reference points.
  2. Find Secret unlocks earlier than any other form.
  3. Reveals more secret locations per session than other forms.
  4. Detects ancient, forgotten, or sealed sites specifically.
  5. Maps the full layout of a dungeon or estate upon entry.

Hourglass, Tracks elapsed time at base. Specializes in Deep Down and timed event awareness.

  1. Displays an accurate countdown to the next cooldown reset.
  2. Deep Down unlocks earlier than other forms.
  3. Warns when a timed quest or world event is about to expire.
  4. Slows enemy pursuit for one round per session.
  5. Rewinds one failed skill roll per session, one chance to try again.

Spyglass, Magnifies distant vision at base. Longest alert range of all forms.

  1. Scouts terrain at range, revealing what's ahead before the party arrives.
  2. Alert triggers at greater distance than any other form.
  3. Identifies creature type and rough power LVL at range.
  4. Detects traps and ambushes before the party enters range.
  5. Marks a distant target, the Wayfarer ports its owner directly to it.

Monocle, Inspects nearby objects at base. Specializes in identification and social intelligence.

  1. Reveals hidden properties or value of inspected objects.
  2. Identifies quest NPCs on sight before any conversation.
  3. Reads and translates languages and scripts automatically.
  4. Detects disguised or hidden creatures within close range.
  5. Reveals the true identity of any creature it is focused on.

Lantern, Provides light at base. Strongest fire bond and group utility at high LVLs.

  1. Functions in magical darkness where other light sources fail.
  2. Ports to fire sources at greater distance than other forms.
  3. Hearth bond strengthens, the owner regenerates faster while resting at a Hearth.
  4. Reveals invisible creatures within its light radius.
  5. Group port, beacons to all party members simultaneously, bringing the whole party along.

Signet Ring, Marks identity and ownership at base. Specializes in Mark Spot and long-range signaling.

  1. Marks ownership of a location or object, others can see it is claimed.
  2. Can hold two marked spots simultaneously instead of one.
  3. Sends a signal to another ring-bearer across any distance.
  4. Can hold four marked spots simultaneously.
  5. Permanently claims a location, no other Player may mark it.

Orrery, Models nearby celestial bodies at base. Specializes in All Aboard and vessel detection.

  1. Displays planet and moon positions in the local system.
  2. All Aboard unlocks earlier than other forms.
  3. Predicts orbital windows for spacecraft boarding.
  4. Detects vessels within range before they become visible.
  5. Locks onto any vessel in the system regardless of distance.

Runestone Disc, Channels rune energy at base. Specializes in rune and Relic detection.

  1. Pulses near rune-inscribed objects, armaments, and structures.
  2. Detects nearby Runesmith NPCs or services.
  3. Pulses near hidden Relic fragments.
  4. Identifies rune type and strength on any object without a Runesmith.
  5. Activates dormant runes on objects, no Runesmith required.

Example, 5 ft. wide, 8 ft. tall boulder in a 10 ft. ceiling dungeon

  • Small, Can pass alongside the boulder freely. Climbing over is possible but the gap between the top of the boulder and the ceiling is only 2 ft., a tight space requiring a Squeeze check to clear.
  • Standard, Can pass alongside but is at the edge of their minimum. Climbing over is blocked, the 2 ft. ceiling gap is impassable for their size. Must go around.

  • Goliath, Impassable in all directions. Requires destruction or an alternate route.

Item Rank Requirements

A Character's ability to use an item is governed by two factors: whether they can physically carry it (Strength and Encumbrance), and whether they meet the item's Rank requirement. At character creation and throughout their career, a Character may carry any item up to their total carry capacity. However, they may only use (equip, wield, or activate) an item if they meet that item's minimum Rank requirement.

Rank Requirements by Item Type: Weapons, Armor, Armaments, and Lore Cards all carry a rank designation (Common, Uncommon, Rare, Unique, Heroic, Elite, Legendary, Divine). A Character must be at the same rank as the item to use it, with one exception: Common rank items can be used by Characters of any rank.

Movement Summary

Movement covers how navigation is determined in SORC, measured in squares on a grid where scale depends on the map type, 5 ft. per square on a battle map, larger on war and world maps. Base speed is determined by race size, terrain affects distance, and every movement type from walking to spacecraft has its own speed modifier and rules. Encumbrance (see below), visibility, hazards, traps, and rest all interact with movement in defined ways. Carrying more than 50% of capacity reduces movement speed, see Encumbrance for full penalty values.

From crawling and climbing to flying mounts, teleportation, and light speed travel, every movement type is accounted for, with organic and non-organic equivalents paired across the system. Abilities can modify or negate most penalties, and the GM Codex covers advanced cases. Reminder: movement in structured encounters consumes Stamina, see Stamina and Mana Costs under Actions for details.

Travel and Recovery

Traveling encompasses long-distance journeys across land, sea, and air. Recovery and downtime requirements depend on travel method and distance covered. Characters can participate in normal activities, downtime, rest periods, and even combat, during vehicular journeys, but foot and mount travel have specific fatigue thresholds tied to Endurance.

Complex Rule Set only.

Light Speed Travel: Characters using Light Speed travel require a long day of uninterrupted rest after arrival to fully recover. Some spacecraft have dedicated resting areas to facilitate this recovery during long journeys.

Warp, Teleportation: Characters using Warp teleportation require 2 hours of downtime or light activity after arrival to recover from the magical strain.

Foot Travel: Characters traveling on foot are limited to 10 miles before requiring a Breather to recover. After every 10 miles traveled OR when the Character exhausts their daily Endurance, whichever comes first, downtime is required. See Rest section for Breather durations and recovery amounts.

Vehicular Travel: Characters traveling by train, by wagon, by airship, or by sea ship can participate in normal activities during the journey, downtime, rest periods, and even combat encounters. The vehicle's movement is independent of character actions. Long-distance vehicular journeys may have specified rest requirements depending on journey length and conditions, determined by the GM.

Land Mounts: Characters traveling on land mounts can journey longer distances based on mount speed and rarity tier. After every 50 miles traveled, the rider and mount require a Breather to recover. Mount pace is determined by rarity: Rare and below (3x base speed), Unique through Elite (4x base speed), Legendary (5x base speed), Divine (6x base speed). See Rest section for Breather durations and recovery amounts.

Travel Example, Tredekian Caravan Ambush

Setup: A merchant caravan travels the trade roads of Tredeki. After 12 miles of wagon travel, they pause at a waystation for a Breather. The party members are traveling by wagon (Vehicular Travel) with merchants and guards. As the caravan exits the station for the next leg of journey, bandits attempt a hijacking.

Outcome: Combat erupts on the wagon-road. Since this is vehicular travel, the party can participate in combat encounters during the journey without penalty. The battle is resolved using standard combat rules. If the party defeats the bandits, the caravan continues. If they lose, the merchants are robbed but the party can still recover. The journey itself is not interrupted, vehicular travel allows for independent action during movement. The caravan will require additional Breathers based on total distance traveled and GM discretion regarding the incident.

Travel Example, Ignis Train Derailment Emergency

Setup: The party rides a steam train through Ignis's volcanic plateaus. Halfway through a 40-mile journey, a massive lava surge causes the tracks to buckle. Combat encounters occur as the party works to stabilize the train or escape.

Outcome: Like wagon travel, the train allows full combat participation during the journey. The party must roll initiative and engage the natural disaster or enemies attempting to cause the derailment. Success means they stabilize the train and continue. Failure means the train crashes, survival depends on a series of checks. Either way, the journey is disrupted. The GM determines if the party must take a longer Breather or find shelter in Ignis's ruins to recover before continuing.

Travel Example, Omnè-Zailister Airship Trade Route

Setup: The party travels by luxury airship from Omnè's floating platforms to Zailister's orbital docks, a journey of 200+ miles taking several days. Multiple vendors, quest-givers, and NPCs are aboard. The party has access to merchant stalls, a small tavern, and crew quarters.

Outcome: During vehicular journeys like this, the party can engage in downtime activities, shopping from vendors, accepting quests from NPCs, conducting research, or simply resting. Combat can occur if the airship encounters pirates or environmental hazards. The journey's duration is fixed by the GM, but party activities make the transit narratively rich. When the airship docks on Zailister, the party emerges refreshed and resupplied.

Travel Example, Light Speed Jump to Tredeki

Setup: A divine entity with Blink Ability, or a light-speed-capable spacecraft, instantly travels from one location to Tredeki across the void. Light Speed travel is instantaneous with no intervening time.

Recovery Requirement: Upon arrival, the travelers require a long day of uninterrupted rest. If traveling by spacecraft, the vessel has dedicated resting quarters. Characters cannot take actions, participate in additional combat, or engage in strenuous activities during this recovery period. The deep exhaustion from FTL travel demands complete rest. Only after this full day can the party proceed normally.

Travel Example, Glaciera Foot Travel Ten-Mile Threshold

Setup: The party travels on foot across Glaciera's frozen wastes. Each character has 35 Endurance points. After 10 miles or when a character exhausts their daily Endurance (whichever comes first), they must stop.

Resolution: After 10 miles, the party requires a Breather (1-2 hours of downtime, see Rest section). A Breather recovers Life, Mana, and Stamina, and restores Endurance to 60% of max (+1%/Spirit point, capped 100%). Endurance still fully resets at the start of the next day regardless. If characters attempt to push past this threshold without a Breather, they risk exhaustion penalties determined by the GM. After a Breather, the party can continue another 10 miles.

Travel Example, Tredeki Mount Journey Long Distance

Setup: A rider on a Unique-tier mount (4x base speed) travels across Tredeki's vast plains. The mount is a bonded creature with Unique rarity. The rider wants to cover 100 miles.

Distance and Breathers: Mount pace: 4x base speed (if base mount speed is 30 ft., effective speed becomes 120 ft. per round). For long-distance travel, the GM determines pace narratively. After every 50 miles, the rider and mount require a Breather to recover. So: 50 miles, first Breather (1-2 hours). 50 more miles, second Breather. The 100-mile journey requires two Breathers minimum, broken into segments. Without these Breathers, mount and rider accumulate exhaustion and risk injury or abandonment.

Travel Example, Omnè Warp Escape from Danger

Setup: The party faces overwhelming odds in a dungeon on Omnè. A character with the Warp innate ability warps the party to safety, a location 200+ miles away, instantly.

Recovery Requirement: After arrival, the party requires 2 hours of downtime or light activity to recover from the magical strain of warping. During this time, they can rest, tend wounds (via medical items or abilities), or prepare for the next encounter, but they cannot engage in strenuous activity or combat. After 2 hours, they are free to continue their journey.

Combat

Armaments Reminder: A character readies up to 3 weapons at the start of each game day; swapping between readied weapons in combat costs an Action (a turn) to sheath or draw. Armor is part of that same daily Armament Set and cannot be donned or doffed mid-session, it's fixed until the next game day. See Playable Classes, pg. 3, and Weapons Carried, pg. 4, for the full readying rules. Characters can still hone their skills on crafts and hobbies, utilizing their Endurance for the remaining time of the day, during their downtime waiting out the 24 hour readied weapon cooldown.

Utilizing a D100 percentile system, to determine combat outcomes, Players describe actions and roll dice to attack with weapons and their combat Abilities to reach the threshold of their target's total Protection Score (PROTS). Every Character's hit and damage is dependent on the rank of the weapon they are using.

Note on Bosses, Mobs, and Companions in Combat: Bosses, Mobs, and Companions do not have Traits, ATTRs, Mana, Stamina, or Endurance. They only have Life, Abilities, Armor, and ATTKs. Their Abilities are innate and require no trait or attribute Prerequisites. Only Life can be depleted during combat.

Basic Attacks and Ability Attacks

Every attack in SORC is one of two things, and they follow different rules about what may be in hand.

A Basic ATTK is the plain swing, thrust, loose, or throw a weapon was built for, carrying no Ability, costing no Stamina or Mana, and available to every Character every turn. No class, Path, or Branch restricts a Basic ATTK. Any Character may pick up any weapon they are physically able to wield, a Magic User with a greatsword, an Avenger with a bow, and attack with it normally, rolling d100 + applicable modifiers against the target's PROTS the same as anyone else. The only things governing a Basic ATTK are the weapon's own Type and Style damage modifiers (see Weapon Type DMG Mods, pg. 4) and whatever the Character can actually carry.

An Ability ATTK is different. Every Tier, Path, and Branch lists the weapons its Abilities are performed with, and one of those must be readied to use any Ability from that tree (see Required Weapons, Appendix). A weapon the tree does not list, or one its Culture names Forbidden, performs none of that tree's Abilities. This is not a penalty, a scaling modifier, or something a high Trait score overcomes; the Ability is simply not available with the wrong thing in hand.

Why the difference: the restriction is one of Culture, not capability (see Cultural Armaments & Rarity, Appendix, and Culture, Playable Races, pg. 2). A Cleric's training, rites, and holy symbols are built around blunt weapons of ordinary make; the same Cleric is perfectly capable of swinging a TAW mace, but their faith does not channel through manufactured technology, so no Cleric Ability comes out of it. They may still swing it all day as a Basic ATTK. The classic case: a Character can use a Bow, but cannot perform Cleric Abilities with a Bow.

Readying: because an Ability needs its weapon in hand, the daily Armament Set matters. A Character readies up to three weapons at the start of each game day and swapping between them mid-combat costs an Action (see Armaments Reminder, above, and Weapons Carried, pg. 4). A Character drawing on several Class Trees should ready weapons that cover each tree they intend to use that day, or accept that some of their Abilities are unavailable until they swap.

Armor is not the same: armor never blocks an Ability. Wearing heavier than a tree's Armor Grade threshold costs a scaling Armament Penalty instead, and Traits can reduce it (see Armament Penalty, Appendix).

Condition: Every piece of armor carries a Condition, rolled on d100 when it is found or acquired, and Condition adjusts Armor Score as a percentage of the piece's Grade rather than as flat points, so wear costs the same share of protection at every Grade.

PROTS Formula: PROTS = Base Armor Scr. + Defense or Resistance Trait (depending on attack source) + shield Armor Scr. + all other modifiers (buffs, debuffs, environmental effects). Against martial or Nature-sourced attacks, use Defense Trait. Against Spell-sourced attacks, use Resistance Trait. These two never stack on the same attack, exactly one applies based on the attack's source.

Every entity in SORC has a Base Armor Score (Armor Scr.), a number between 1 and 100 representing the raw protection of any armored or unarmored entity. Armor Scr. is the starting point, not the full picture. PROTS (Protection Score) is Armor Scr. plus every permanent Enhancement or Impairment, temporary buff or debuff, and Trait bonus or penalty applied to it. PROTS = Armor Scr. + all modifiers, positive and negative. Armor Scr. never changes unless the armor itself changes. PROTS fluctuates with buffs, debuffs, Enhancements, Impairments, and situational modifiers.

Example: Ice Dragon, on the ground, has a base Armor Scr. of 80 (Armor Scr. 80) and has 12 bonuses to Protection, so its PROTS 92. Rolling 1D100 would need a result of 92 to Hit the dragon and roll for damage. In the air, the Ice Dragon gets a +10 bonus, now giving it +22 (12 + 10) bonus to its base Armor Scr. 80. Flying, the Ice Dragon has a PROTS of 102.

Conversely, a Commoner is wearing cloth and has a base Armor Scr. of 10, Armor Scr. 10, but has part of a wooden gate he's used as a shield giving him +2, however he doesn't have a strong foothold because of the environment, -1 (strong winds). His total PROTS is Armor Scr. 10 + 2 - 1 = 11. The Commoner is easy to Hit. In comparison, a Paladin in Osmium Plate has a base Armor Scr. of 60. If he's holding Bracers, a 2h Weapon or a Shield he can add his Defense score (Defense is a Trait) representing; Parry or Block respectively he'll add his Protection, in this case 4. He also gets the base Armor Scr. of a Shield (Armor Scr. 10) or the lighter option, Bracers (Armor Scr. 5). Let's say he's holding the shield and has a score of 5 on the Trait Defense. Base Armor Scr. is 60, plus 10 for shield, plus 5 because he has Defense (5).

Defense Roll

Defense Trait is passively included in PROTS and does not require a roll. If the attacker's score meets or exceeds the target's total PROTS (which includes the Defense Trait, Base Armor Scr., shield Armor Scr., and all other modifiers), the attack lands. Defense type is determined by equipped gear:

  • Parry, 2H Weapon, Bracers, or Dual Wield (melee only, excludes orbs, tomes, special items, and environmental objects). Bracers allow Parry, Gloves do not (see Set Bonus, pg. 4)
  • Block, Shield
  • Dodge, Light Armor

Characters without the Defense Trait or qualifying gear cannot use Defense. See Defense under Traits (pg. 2).

Defense Example, Omnè Crystal Lock Difficulty Score Check

Setup: Kael (Elf, Thief, Shadowblade, LVL 11, Rank Uncommon) attempts to pick an ancient crystal lock guarding a vault on Omnè's floating platform. The lock has a PROTS of 45 (representing its structural integrity). Kael has the Disarm Trap talent with a score of 8 and Dexterity Trait of 6.

Attempt: Kael rolls to disable: d100 + 8 (talent) + 6 (Dexterity) = [62] + 14 = 76 versus DIFS of 45. Success! He carefully manipulates the crystalline mechanism, feeling the subtle shifts in energy. The lock clicks open, revealing the chamber beyond. No damage taken.

Comparison, If He Failed: If his roll had been [28] + 14 = 42 (below 45), the trap would trigger. Standard trap damage on failure might be 16 Life damage. But if Kael had chosen to destroy the lock instead (attacking its PROTS directly), failure would deal 2x that damage (32 Life) back to him. The Disarm Trap approach was clearly safer.

Defense Example, Zailister Spaceship Security Code Difficulty Score

Setup: Tyx (Goliath, Hacker, DataMind, LVL 13, Rank Rare) tries to bypass a security panel on a Zailister orbital station. The system's defensive DIFS is 58 (representing firewall strength). Tyx has the Hack talent with score 12, Intellect Trait of 10, and a +5 bonus from their specialized hacking equipment.

Attempt: Tyx rolls: d100 + 12 (Hack talent) + 10 (Intellect) + 5 (equipment) = [71] + 27 = 98 versus DIFS of 58. Critical success! The panel sparks briefly as Tyx gains access, bypassing multiple layers of encryption in seconds. The security override code unlocks adjacent doors. Tyx's Hack talent makes this look easy.

Ranged Attacks

Ranged combat covers any attack delivered from a distance using projectiles, energy, or magical effects. All ranged attacks use the d100 roll-over system where your relevant Trait SCR (Agility for weapons, Focus for spells) is added directly to your hit roll. Ranged attacks fall into four primary categories based on their origin: Telluric (bows, crossbows, thrown weapons, and black powder firearms), Technologically Advanced Weapons (TAW) (manufactured technology, splitting into TAW Ballistic and TAW Charged), Chthonic (elemental substance carried around a real weapon, neither cast nor built), and Spell (magical effects cast at range). Each type uses different range bands, damage calculations, and armor interactions.

Telluric Ranged

Telluric projectiles operate under physics-based range mechanics where distance, projectile velocity drops, and stability directly affect accuracy and damage. Distance is measured in feet. (Given: 1 sq. = 5 ft). Every ranged shot, whether arrow, bolt, hand thrown or slung, falls into one of four velocity states based on how far it travels: Point Blank, Oscillation, Stabilized, and Velocity Drop (gravity and air resistance, out to the weapon's maximum range). Band distances past Point Blank vary by weapon class and are read off the table below.

At Point Blank range (0–10 ft., 0–5 ft. for a thrown weapon), if the attacker's d100 roll is a Critical Hit (96-100), it lands automatically and the target's Botch is waived off. The Crit deals 2x damage dice + Agility Score of the attacker (see Critical Hit Formula, Combat, Hit Results, pg. 5). If the attacker's hit roll is a non-critical hit that meets or exceeds the defender's Protection Score (PROTS), the target may attempt a Botch to interrupt it. The target rolls d100 and adds the higher of their Strength Score or Agility Score, plus their Defense Trait Score, against the attacker's total roll, including modifiers. PROTS is not added. A thrown weapon cannot be Botched at any distance. On a meet or exceed the attack is Botched and deals half its damage dice, rounded up, with the attacker's Trait Score still added in full.

Note: Innate Aptitude and Inaptitude apply to this roll; for example, a Gnome with an innate -5 Strength (STR) Inaptitude would add their Agility Score instead of Strength when calculating their defense roll. If the defender's roll fails to meet the attacker's roll, the shot lands uncontested and deals full damage.

Compact Telluric weapons (shortbows, light crossbows, slings, hand cannons, shotguns, blunderbusses, dart guns) reach a maximum of 200 ft. Weapon Families. Telluric, Technologically Advanced Weapons (TAW) and Chthonic are the three power sources a ranged weapon can draw on, and the distinction governs range bands, armor interaction and planetary penalties alike. Telluric is forged or knapped and driven by muscle or mechanism. TAW is technologically manufactured and tuned to the sun of its native planet, losing range off-world. Chthonic is ore and gem out of Ignis, carrying Adoria's own magic, so it is strongest where her light is strongest and takes the same planetary Bonus and Penalty as Magic. All three are defined under Tech and Magic Across Essentia (Planetary Markets, Basic Rules pg. 4).

Reach weapons do not use range bands; they simply extend melee reach to 10 ft. and follow standard melee rules.

Telluric Projectiles

Telluric is by far the most diverse projectile family, spanning four material tiers. A projectile's Grade is the material of the part that strikes, the head, tip or point, not the shaft, so an ordinary wooden shaft under an Adamantium head is an Adamantium projectile.

  • Knapped and carved: Wooden, Bone, Stone, Obsidian, the oldest projectiles on any world and the only ones every people can make.
  • The forged standard: Steel/Iron, Bronze, Hardened Steel, Chromium, what most bundles are built from.
  • Light metals: Silver, Mithril, Refined Steel, worked for the cut rather than the weight.
  • Dense and legendary: Tungsten, Osmium, Iridium, Platinum, Ruthenium, Titanium, Vibranium, Adamantium, Dragon Scale, heads that punish the drawing arm as much as the target.

Slope Mod belongs to whatever launches the projectile, the arm or the bow, and is read off the launcher's own rules (see Slope Conditions, below). Full Grades, damage, bundle sizes and costs are on each Projectile's Card (see Projectile Grade, pg. 4).

TAW Ranged

Technologically Advanced Weapons (TAW) are rail guns, mini guns, high velocity sniper rifles, laser pistols, plasma pistols and rifles, plasma grenades that stick, and other technologically advanced weaponry originally manufactured on the planets further out from Adoria. TAW splits into two: TAW Ballistic, which fires a physical round, and TAW Charged, which fires light or plasma. A laser pistol or a plasma cannon is TAW Charged, not Chthonic, because it was built (see Chthonic Weapons, pg. 4).

TAW also fields shotguns, and they fire more than plasma. A TAW shotgun will throw minerals much the way lead is thrown from an Telluric gun, but it never burns black powder to do it. The propellant for any TAW round that is not light or plasma is one of two systems: nuclear pulse propulsion or an electrothermal-chemical (ETC) charge. That distinction, powder against pulse or ETC, is the line between an Telluric firearm and a TAW one.

TAW Ranged weapons use range bands and Focus Score for penalty absorption. They are precision-engineered for the atmospheric conditions, gravity, and environmental properties of their native planet. On foreign planets, where gravity, pressure, and atmosphere differ, effective range drops significantly. Band distances are listed on each TAW card and override the defaults below.

A silencer reduces TAW Ranged noise to near-bow level but does not eliminate muzzle flash, ammo dependency, or malfunction risk. TAW Ranged supports burst and automatic fire, used for suppression, which pins enemies in cover without requiring a direct hit. Full suppression and overwatch rules are in the Combat section.

Chthonic Ranged

A Chthonic weapon is worked from ore or gem taken out of Ignis, where Adoria's magic collects in the veins and the ore that runs in them becomes magic with them. The weapon is ordinary craft; what makes it Chthonic is the stone it was made from.

Chthonic is neither cast nor manufactured. No spell puts the magic there and no factory does, the ground did, before anyone dug it out. Because what it carries is Adoria's own magic, it is strongest where her light is strongest and takes the same planetary Bonus and Penalty as Magic.

Chthonic Ranged uses range bands and Focus Score for penalty absorption, and bypasses certain armor types that resist ballistic damage. It carries no planetary Penalty of any kind, and Slope Mod (see Slope Conditions, below) does not touch it either: what does the harm is the energy, not the weapon carrying it, and energy is no mass for gravity to pull on, so High Ground and Low Ground change neither its range nor its damage.

Close Quarters

Close Quarters is 0 to 5 ft. Every melee strike happens here, and so does every grapple, grab and hold. A Melee attack is a Close Quarters strike, not a shot, and uses Strength.

A Botch and an Escape are made here. Both are covered under Unarmed Combat, below.

Unarmed Combat

Unarmed attacks use no weapon rank. Strength replaces the rank hit bonus.

Hit: d100 + Strength vs PROTS

Damage (main): 1d4 + Strength

Damage (off): 1d2 + Strength

Graze, Critical Hit and Fumble rules apply as normal.

Anything using hands is Main Hand and Off Hand still. There are no bonuses for hands unless the Class, Path or Branch itself or the Ability, such as those from Monk or Brawler, define it.

Elbows, knees and the like have no Main or Off Hand. They are Special Actions, below, unless the Class, Path or Branch itself or the Ability, such as those from Monk or Brawler, define it.

Certain class paths, such as Fighter, Brawler, prefer their bare hands, grappling, and improvising with objects from their environment as weapons. Characters with innate unarmed abilities may have class-specific bonuses that override or stack with these base values.

Special Actions

A Special Action is an unarmed technique used in place of a weapon strike: a knee, an elbow, a headbutt, a grapple, a grab, a shove, a trip. It has no Main Hand or Off Hand.

Hit: d100 + Strength vs the target's threshold score. A Special Action carries no Rank and no Grade, so Strength replaces the rank hit bonus.

Damage: 1d4 + Strength. A grapple, grab, shove or trip deals no damage of its own; it holds or moves the target instead.

Hands: a grapple or a grab requires both hands empty. The rest do not. Empty hands are always Unarmed, Main Hand and Off Hand.

Offensive only. A Special Action is taken on your own turn. A shove or a trip made to create distance is still a Special Action. Responding to something aimed at you is a Botch if the action has not landed, or an Escape if a hold already has.

Grapple and Grab: on a successful grapple or grab, the target is held until they Escape or the attacker releases. Drop damage applies if the target is released or Escapes at height. Specific grab rules, carry height limits and drop damage values are module specific.

Abilities supercede. A Character performing a technique they have learned from a Main Class Tier, Path or Branch is fighting Unarmed and uses that Ability. A Brawler's Strangle or Apprehend is an Ability and is read off its own card. Special Actions are what a Character does without that training.

Botch

A Botch is a target's attempt to interrupt an action being taken against them.

Committed Actions. A Botch may only be attempted against an action that takes a beat before it lands. A 1h strike, an Unarmed strike, or anything already in motion is too fast to Botch.

  • A weapon being drawn or swapped mid-combat.
  • A ranged weapon being drawn.
  • A spell being cast.

In a position to. The target must be within Botch reach, below, aware of the actor, and not Incapacitated, held, or already Escaping.

Against a ranged actor.

What cannot be Botched. Reach cannot be Botched. Melee cannot be Botched. A thrown weapon cannot be Botched at any distance. A Critical Hit cannot be Botched.

The roll: the target rolls d100 and adds the higher of their Strength Score or Agility Score, plus their Defense Trait Score, against the actor's total roll, including modifiers. PROTS is not added.

On a Botch: the action deals half its damage dice, rounded up, with the actor's Trait Score added in full.

Cost: the Botch itself is free and does not count against the target's turn. A target may attempt only one Botch per round. A Critical Hit cannot be Botched.

Order. The attacker attacks. The target may then attempt a Botch, if they are in a position to. The result of the Botch alters the damage. Damage is then dealt.

Example. Two fighters are wrestling. One reaches for a knife; the other may Botch the reach. If the knife comes free and is used the following turn, the target may Botch that attack. At any point in the same exchange the target may instead attempt an Escape. Close Quarters moves fast, and the GM calls what a fighter is in a position to do.

Complex Rule Set only.

Escape

An Escape is a target's attempt to break free of a hold already on them.

The roll: the target rolls d100 + Strength against the holder's threshold score. Agility does not apply.

Cost: an Escape costs a full action, and one Escape attempt is allowed per turn. Drop damage applies if the target Escapes at height.

Complex Rule Set only.

Limb-Specific Targeting (LST)

LST allows a character to aim at a specific body part, head, groin, limbs, or torso, for increased damage at the risk of full exposure.

Complex Rule Set only.

Hit Penalty: Determined by the attacker's Weapon Rank and the area targeted (see LST Chart).

On Hit: Deal bonus damage equal to twice the hit penalty value.

On Miss: The attacker loses all defenses through their next turn and rolls 1d10 for stumble distance (feet). The stumble leaves them open to a counter attack from their target and attacks of opportunity from every enemy adjacent to any square they stumble into, including the square they occupied when the LST was attempted.

Example: A 1d10 result of 1 while surrounded on all 4 sides, the attacker stumbles 1 foot but must defend all 4 attacks of opportunity from the surrounding enemies.

There is no Graze or Critical Hit on an LST attack. Refer to the GM Codex for more on LST.

Threshold Score

A threshold score is the number an action is rolled against when that action is aimed at something living. For any MAC or Character it is their PROTS. This covers grapples, grabs, Botch and Escape attempts, fear effects, and any other roll made against or because of an encounter. A DIFS is the GM-set Difficulty Score for a check with no MAC or Character on the other side of it, a locked door or a cliff face. Mechanical devices such as traps carry a PROTS representing their structural integrity and use that as their threshold score.

Complex Rule Set only.

Grapple and Grab

Grapple and grab are Special Actions and are governed there, along with what a Character may do with their hands full. Breaking free of one is an Escape.

Complex Rule Set only.

Traps and Mechanical Devices

Traps and mechanical devices have a PROTS representing their structural integrity. A character may attempt to disable a trap using the Disarm Trap talent (d100 + talent score vs DIFS). Alternatively, a character may attempt to destroy the device outright by attacking its PROTS directly. Failure to destroy deals twice the trap's damage to the Character, compared to the standard damage on a failed disable attempt. This makes the Disarm Trap talent the safer and preferred approach.

Non-organic items with a DIFS also have a PROTS, but failing to destroy them does more damage.

The Cultist has additional defensive modifiers, a shield, defensive stance, and terrain advantage, bringing his full PROTS to 60. Yura's Hit bonus is +8 (Common rank, Main hand). Underarmor Requirements

Certain armor types require underarmor to be worn beneath them. Underarmor prevents chafing, aids movement, and allows for proper rest while armored. It adds weight to the Character's total carry load.

  • Cloth, Leather, Fur, no underarmor required.
  • Medium (chain, ring mail, etc.), requires cloth underarmor at minimum.
  • Heavy (plate, etc.), requires faulding at minimum. Faulding is a padded garment worn beneath heavy armor to absorb impact and prevent joint wear.
  • Dense / Composite (dragon scale, etc.), requires a gambeson at minimum. A gambeson is a thick quilted and padded jacket, heavier than faulding, necessary to bear the weight and rigidity of the densest armors.

Wearing armor without the required underarmor results in movement penalties, reduced rest recovery, and chafing penalties determined by the GM.

Armor Grades

Armor Taxonomy. Grade, Mould, Material and Type are four separate things, and an armor is only fully described once all four are named. The Armor Mould (often just Mould) is the specific build a piece of armor is worked into, Plate, Leather, Chain, Dragon Scale, Osmium Plate. The Material is what it is made from, Hide for Leather and Fur Moulds, Steel and Iron, Osmium and Tungsten, Mithril, Dragon Scale and so on, and the material is what sets the Grade. The same Mould can sit at different Grades depending on its Material, a Steel Plate is Heavy Grade while an Osmium Plate is Dense Grade. Names in the rules read Grade + Material + Mould, so a "Composite Dragon Scale Plate" is Composite Grade, Dragon Scale Material, Plate Mould. Type is a fourth and separate axis entirely, Martial, Magic, TAW, and Chthonic.

Combat Movement

Dodge and Retreat: Combines Dodge-, Agility SCR and Dexterity SCR to PROTS with a 5 ft. sq. Retreat backwards. Costs 1 action. Does not trigger an ATTK of Opportunity. Defense Trait passively applies to PROTS during this action.

ATTKs of Opportunity: Triggered when a hostile creature moves out of a character's melee reach without using Retreat or Dodge and Retreat, or when a creature moves into their melee reach. ATTKs of Opportunity work both ways. Additionally, performing certain actions while in a hostile creature's melee reach also triggers an ATTK of Opportunity against the Character, including casting a spell, using a ranged weapon, using an item, or standing from prone. Free action, costs no actions. One ATTK of Opportunity per trigger, per turn. Roll: d100 + Hit bonus vs target PROTS. Standard damage on hit.

Flanking: A target is flanked when two hostile creatures are adjacent to them on opposite sides. A flanked target loses their Defense Trait from PROTS until they are no longer flanked; they cannot actively defend two directions at once. Flanking requires no action and no roll.

Cover: Behind cover you are out of the attacker's line of sight and they have no visible target.

Prone, Unconscious, Incapacitated, Dying and Dead

Prone: A character who is prone must spend half an action to stand. While prone, melee attacks against them gain +10 to the roll. Ranged attacks against them take -10. Crawling while prone costs 1/4 speed.

Unconscious: The character is prone and cannot act. All attacks against them automatically hit, no roll required. They cannot defend and remain unconscious until stabilized or restored by a Nature or Spell Ability.

Incapacitated: The character is prone or immobile and cannot take actions or reactions. ATTKs against them gain +10. They may still be conscious but are unable to act. See Downtime.

Dying: The character is prone and losing Life each round. At the start of each of their turns they lose 1 Life Chip, tracked with purple Chips once Life drops below zero. They may attempt a Fortitude (FRT) check at a DIFS set by the GM, a success stabilizes them and stops the bleed. A failure continues the bleed. If Life reaches -(10 + Constitution score), the Character must go through rejuvenation, see Resurrection, Revitalize and Rejuvenation below. Allies may heal or stabilize a dying character before this threshold is reached, another character may attempt this with 1 action and a successful check, specified by module or determined by the GM.

Dead: At -(10 + Constitution score) Life the Character is prone and takes no further actions. Their turn is skipped. Allies may still attempt revival or resurrection, see Resurrection, Revitalize and Rejuvenation. Effects on the body are specified by module or determined by the GM.

Other entities can aid unconscious, dying or dead characters.

See Resurrection, Revitalize and Rejuvenation.

More on Downtime

Dying in SORC does not necessarily mean a Character will die permanently. There are different penalties for dying in succession, and the nature of death or crimes will determine the specific consequences. The penalties for death and other sustaining conditions such as comatosis, prison stays, infirmary stays, hobbies, and crafting are calculated using the same formula:

Penalty Time = Base Time * (Growth Rate)^(Nature of Offenses)

This formula applies to the penalties for dying, crimes, and related circumstances, with base times varying depending on the specific offense or condition.

Incapacitation and Recovery: Being in comatosis, prison, or infirmary, and the time spent on hobbies or crafting all constitute periods of downtime that can affect a character's progression.

Penalties for Death and Crimes: Penalties are determined by the formula above, factoring in the severity of the offense or circumstance, which can include jail time, fines, or other restrictions.

Other Downtime Activities: Time spent in various states of incapacitation or recovery impacts gameplay and character development, with specific durations and penalties tailored to each situation.

Note: Crafting and Profession-based activities are resolved using the Crafting Resolution mechanic (see Skills section). Characters engage in downtime crafting during rest periods between adventures.

Work While Away (Between Sessions)

Essentia doesn't pause just because the table does. Whatever stretch of real time passes between one session and the next, every character is assumed to have spent it living somewhere, most often their declared Home (see Home and Family, Basic Rules pg. 4), a ship being one of the most common choices for a party that travels together rather than settles down. Before a session ends, a Player tells the GM what their character intends to do with that time, crafting, running a Profession, training, managing a business, tending relationships, or anything else that fits the Character and their Home.

Complex Rule Set only.

The Cap: No matter how much real-world time actually passes, Work While Away only ever covers one in-game day's worth of that character's Endurance, spent only on non-combat activity that calls for a roll (crafting, Profession work, Talent or Skill checks, and the like). A character isn't quietly living out a whole week of progress between two Tuesday sessions, they're living out one day, the same as if that day had been played at the table.

Resolution: Nothing is rolled between sessions. At the start of the next session, the GM resolves every declared task as a batch, a roll fest of Crafting Resolution checks (see Skills) and whatever other checks the declared work calls for, spending that character's Endurance exactly as it would spend in play, until either the declared tasks run out or the day's Endurance does, whichever comes first. Successes, failures, goods produced, and gold spent or earned are all narrated and applied before the new session's action begins.

No Declaration, No Roll: A character with nothing on file from their Player is simply assumed to have rested, maintained their gear, and gone about ordinary life, no rolls, no gains, no losses. Work While Away rewards a Player who plans ahead, it never penalizes one who didn't.

The World Keeps Moving: Work While Away isn't only about a single character's Endurance. NPCs act on their own agendas, factions shift, markets rise and fall, rumors spread, and earlier Dilemma and Standing choices ripen into consequences, all whether or not any character's declared task touches them. The GM is encouraged to bring at least one such development into the next session regardless of what, if anything, the party asked for.

Missing Players: A Player who can't make a session doesn't pull their character out of the world. Another Player at the table, or the GM, runs that character for the session, guided by the absent Player's notes and known intentions, including whatever they declared for that session's Work While Away. Parties can also recruit a substitute Player online to sit in for a session, see SORC Web, Basic Rules pg. 1.

Death Penalty

Dying carries lasting consequences beyond the immediate danger of combat. When a character dies and is later revived, whether through Resurrection, Revitalize, or Rejuvenation, the following penalties apply:

Time Lost: Recovery follows the same formula used for comatosis, prison, and infirmary stays (see More on Downtime above), Penalty Time = Base Time × (Growth Rate)^(Nature of Offenses), with each successive death by the same character increasing the exponent.

LVL Loss: The character loses exactly 1 Growth, Current Years (EA) resets to that Growth's starting year threshold, and exactly 1 Character LVL (LVL). This loss does not escalate with repeat deaths; only the Time Lost above does. ATTR SCRs, TST, and Innate Aptitude gains already purchased or picked are kept in full, nothing is refunded or reverted. Instead, the Character does not roll a new ATTR Repository or pick a new class Ability again until their LVL exceeds the LVL they died at, and does not gain a new Innate Aptitude pick again until their Growth exceeds the Growth they died at. Climbing back to either one is free re-earning with no further reward attached; only surpassing it resumes normal progression.

Rank: See Rank and Death on pg. 4, Rank is capped by the Character's reduced LVL and restores automatically once that LVL is reached again. Rank PTS already earned are never lost.

Armaments: Roll 1d4 for each readied armament that carries at least one Enhancement (Combining, Enchanting, Inscribing, Modifying, Nesting, Reforging, Resonating, Socketing, or Upgrading, see Enhancing and Modification Armaments, pg. 4). That many Enhancements are stripped at random from the item; if the roll exceeds the number present, all of them are stripped. The base armament, its condition, and its materials are unaffected, this penalty removes applied work only, and never breaks or destroys the item outright.

Resurrection, Revitalize and Rejuvenation

Note: Revitalize and Rejuvenation, including their checks, windows, and DIFS values below, are currently under development and subject to change.

Complex Rule Set only.

Resurrection: Resurrection is the process of being brought back to life after dying. Many different classes can perform it, including some exclusive to NPCs. Resurrection does not directly alter alignment, though a character's choices before and after resurrection can shift their alignment as play continues. Depending on how the character died and how they were resurrected, they may receive a Shamed Title (see Undead Shamed Titles, below) which may trigger Fallen or Risen mechanics if they are devoted to a Higher Power.

Revitalize: Revitalization is the process of stopping the new effects of how a character died from continuing. It begins the instant a character dies to a corrupting source (undead, fiend, curse, or similar, the GM determines which deaths qualify). The character has a window equal to the Time Lost above to Revitalize. Once per GM-set interval within that window (such as each long rest), the Character rolls d100 + Willpower or Piety, whichever best resists the nature of the corruption, GM's call, against a DIFS set by the strength of what killed them. Success halts the corruption; the Character proceeds to a standard Resurrection and takes the Death Penalty above, nothing further. If the window expires without success, the Character fully transforms into whatever killed them, such as becoming fully Undead, and is removed from play as a PC. The GM may continue them as an NPC, possibly hunted by their own former party, or otherwise loose in the world.

Rejuvenation: Rejuvenation requires Rank Hero, Growth 30, and the aid of a powerful Cleric. It is said to cost everything a character has, including their standing and surname, in truth, this is just how people describe the ordinary Death Penalty above, which already applies to the death that made Rejuvenation possible in the first place. Rejuvenation itself does not further affect Traits, Abilities, or ATTRs, and Charisma actually increases. The binding requires three rolls, d100 + Piety against a DIFS the Cleric sets, scaled to the Character's Rank, and all three must succeed. On success, the Character Rejuvenates as a hybrid race, either Subdead or Subdivine, retaining all Abilities learned before and gaining the new benefits of the hybrid Sub. The Higher Power governing a Subdead or Subdivine character is determined by whichever Path they've invested in has progressed furthest; if two or more Paths are tied for furthest progression, the GM decides which Higher Power governs the Character. Continuing as either afterward requires a long chain quest throughout their respective Higher Power, tracked through their continued Growth. On failure, any of the three rolls comes up short, the Character loses its Sub status and takes a second full Death Penalty (this is the "significant amount of Experience (XP)" at stake), but retains their original alignment and Higher Power devotion (if any) from before the Rejuvenation attempt.

Undead Shamed Titles: A GM may issue an Undead-flavored Shamed Title (such as Graveborn, Husk-Touched, or the Hollow) to a character who fails to Revitalize in time, undergoes Rejuvenation, or otherwise returns from death visibly wrong. This uses the standard Shamed Title rules (see Titles under Prestige), it dissipates through good deeds, grows harder to remove if bad deeds continue, and counts toward the accumulated Boasted and Exposed Titles that can trigger Fallen or Risen mechanics organically.

Turns, Rounds and Time

As long as they are not incapacitated, every character, in the order of initiative, gets a turn each round. A turn represents 6 seconds of in-game time. A round is complete when every character in the encounter has taken their turn.

Actions

Every character has a base of 5 actions per turn. A BASE combat strike costs 1 action. Movement and subtle actions, like sheathing a weapon, each cost half an action. Base defense, communicative actions like a whisper, shout, or gesture, drops, and Traits do not cost actions.

For every 2 points in the Agility Trait, a Character gains 5 ft. of additional free movement per turn, this does not cost an action. For example, a Bast has a Goliath base speed of 40 ft. and begins with +2 innate Agility, granting them an additional 5 ft. of free movement (45 ft. base for Bast) each turn. Conversely, for every 1 point of negative Agility, a character loses 5 ft. of base movement per turn.

Combat Abilities, both offensive and defensive, vary in action cost depending on their power, each is listed on its respective card. Talents and crafting skills follow the same rule, with cost specified on the card.

Certain Abilities, Traits, and conditions, both beneficial and harmful, can modify action costs up or down respectively. These modifiers are noted on the card.

Uses Cap, Per Turn: No Ability may be used more than once during the same character's own Turn, regardless of its Uses Tier or how much Vitality remains to pay for it. This is an absolute rule, not a Vitality-derived one, an Ability with Uses: Unlimited or 3/Round is still capped at one use inside any single Turn, the Round- and Day-scale Uses Tiers on an Ability Card govern how many Turns across a Round or a full day it can be used, never how many times within the same Turn.

Stamina and Mana Costs

Martial Abilities consume Stamina chips when used, with the cost specified on each card. Spells consume Mana chips when cast, with the cost specified on each spell card. Movement in structured encounters (combat and exploration grids) consumes Stamina chips equal to 1 chip per the Character's base speed per turn, plus 1 additional chip for every 5 feet moved beyond that base speed in the same turn. Overland travel does not incur Stamina costs. A character cannot use a Martial Ability, cast a spell, or move in structured encounters if they lack the required Stamina or Mana respectively. Both pools regenerate each turn based on Spirit (every 5 Spirit = 1 Chip of regeneration per turn). Traits apply passively at no Stamina cost (see Traits vs. Talents, pg. 2). Talents and Skills are never used in combat, see Talent Card and Skill Card, below.

Movement Example, Tredekian Battlefield Advance

Setup: Gregor (Human, Paladin, Holy Knight, LVL 15, Rank Uncommon) has a base speed of 30 ft. per turn in combat on Tredeki's scarred battlefields. He has 42 Stamina Chips total and starts the turn with 35 available. He wants to dash across the field to reach a distant enemy.

Movement Cost Calculation: Gregor's base speed is 30 ft. Movement cost = 1 chip per base speed + 1 per 5 feet beyond. His base speed costs 1 chip. He wants to move 50 ft. (20 feet beyond his base 30 ft.). That's 4 additional increments of 5 feet = 4 extra chips. Total cost: 1 + 4 = 5 Stamina chips.

Resolution: Gregor pays 5 Stamina (down to 30 available). He charges 50 ft. across the Tredekian battlefield, his armor clanging with each thundering step. He arrives at his destination ready for combat, though having expended significant stamina reserves for this aggressive advance.

Movement Example, Omnè Crystal Cavern Precision Navigation

Setup: Sira (Small race, Scout, Tracker, LVL 12, Rank Rare) navigates through Omnè's narrow crystal caverns. Her base speed is 25 ft. per turn (Small racial size). Stamina available: 28 of 40. The cavern terrain is rough (half speed = 12 ft. base movement for this turn).

Movement Cost Calculation: Due to rough terrain reducing speed to half, Sira's effective base speed becomes 12 ft. for this terrain type. She wants to move 20 ft. to avoid falling crystals. Cost = 1 chip for base rough terrain movement (12 ft.) + 1 chip for the additional 8 feet (less than 5 more, but the rule is per 5 feet, so this is 1 additional chip). Total: 2 Stamina chips.

Resolution: Sira deftly navigates the treacherous crystal formations, paying only 2 Stamina (down to 26). She reaches safety as a massive crystal spike crashes where she'd been standing moments before. Her training as a Scout makes precise movement through hazardous terrain second nature.

Time

The GM tracks in-game time through rounds, converting to minutes and hours as needed. Between encounters, the GM advances a clock, no action counting, just narrative time passing.

Game Engine Cards

Core components that drive the gameplay mechanics, including Action Cards for combat and skills, Narrative Cards for storytelling and interactions, and Dilemma Cards for tough choices. These Cards facilitate smooth, dynamic gameplay and strategic decision-making throughout a character's adventure. The following is a list of Game Engine Card variants.

Action (Category)

Actions take stamina and Endurance. Characters need to distribute their actions meaningfully. Each character only gets a certain amount of actions per day, determined by endurance. Stamina determines the amount of actions a character can take on their turn, with a cap and some free actions.

Action Cards come in four distinct types: Martial Abilities, Spell Abilities, Talent Cards, and Skill Cards. As characters face combat threats, they'll present a Martial Ability or a Spell Ability at the GM's request, the Card played must be relevant to the situation and within their own capabilities of using the Card at that time, for instance whether or not they have the Vitality (stamina, mana etc) in order to take the action. Martial Abilities and Spell Abilities are both Combat Action Cards but are entirely separate types, they are not interchangeable. Talent Cards and Skill Cards are never played in combat, see each below.

Martial Ability: Covers martial and physical combat actions. Players keep a stack of Martial Abilities representing their characters' combat capabilities for quick reference.

Spell Ability: Covers magical combat actions. Spell Abilities are separate from Martial Abilities. Each Spell Ability specifies its own Mana cost and effects, these vary per card and are not determined by ATTRs.

Talent Card (talents): Requires challenge, see talents. This is how Players reference their talents.

Skill Card, Recipe Card (professions): Must be out of combat. Requires recipe, materials and at least a make-shift workshop, see skills. Threat Engine

A Threat Meter tracks a character's Threat SCR, indicating how much attention they're attracting from Creatures and NPCs. When the score is low, the Character goes mostly unnoticed; a higher score means they're more likely to be observed, confronted, or targeted. Every action, whether escaping, negotiating, hiding, taunting, or distracting, can change a character's Threat SCR.

  • Clever actions and successful deception lower a character's threat score.
  • Aggressive, loud, or risky actions raise a character's threat score.
  • As NPCs or creatures become more hostile or suspicious, a character's Threat SCR increases.
  • If a character placates, impresses, or deceives them, their Threat SCR decreases.

The GM updates a character's Threat SCR based on their actions and how others react, making it dynamic in every Encounter, not just combat.

Panic Engine

Whenever a character faces intense stress, fear, or danger, whether hiding, climbing, running from monsters, escaping pursuit, or negotiating in tense situations, the GM calls for a Panic Check. For the Panic Check, the Character rolls D100. If the total meets or exceeds the difficulty score (DIFS) of the source causing Panic, they remain calm. If not, they begin to panic, risking mistakes or exposure. Panic will grow progressively as Characters take their turns and each LVL will alter the Character more as the panic grows.

Roll a D100 Panic Check and add any modifiers (like the Trait Courage) to attempt meeting the DIFS of whatever is causing the panic. Every hazardous event and entity has a DIFS for Panic, that Difficulty Score is the target number the Player must reach to avoid Panic. If there isn't a DIFS, the GM will create one.

If the Character fails the target DIFS of Panic, they'll begin a progressive/regressive Panic process for each of the Character's turns where Panic is ongoing. For example if a Character fails a Panic Check they are now at Panic LVL 1, and must roll a Panic Check again on their next turn with a -1 Penalty. If they fail again, they are now at LVL 2 Panic and on their next turn with a -2 Penalty to their D100 roll. This continues through 5 LVLs of Panic.

If they succeed a Panic Check, they go back one LVL of Panic, are not panicked anymore, and they can take their free movement action before ending their turn. GM may set or adjust Panic Difficulty based on the situation.

Narrative Event Cards

Narrative Events are used during moments where Players can creatively escape danger or interact with Creatures and NPCs instead of fighting. Each Card presents a situation and encourages inventive solutions, allowing Players to use the environment, combine Abilities, or negotiate with creatures for Unique outcomes.

Dynamic Escape

Some Narrative Cards describe hazardous scenarios. Players can use their surroundings, trigger chain reactions, or combine skills for dynamic escapes. The risk and reward depend on how clever or daring their actions are.

Creature and Non-Player Character Interaction

Other Narrative Cards focus on Encounters with monsters or NPCs. Players may bargain, manipulate behaviors, or incite chaos to avoid combat or gain advantages. The Cards provide prompts for creative negotiation or influence.

Flexible Resolution

The outcome of each action is determined on a scale, such as full escape, partial success, added complications, or failure. Results are not limited to simple success or failure, allowing for nuanced storytelling and consequences.

Player-Driven and Reactive

Players are encouraged to initiate creative actions in response to Narrative Cards. The game Master provides cues and adapts the scenario based on Player choices, making the event dynamic and responsive.

The use of Narrative Cards rewards teamwork, risk-taking, and thinking outside the box. Players are empowered to find Unique solutions to Encounters, and inventive actions can lead to special rewards, new story paths, or lasting changes in the world.

Dilemma Cards

The Dilemma Engine is a central feature that turns tough choices into lasting effects. At pivotal moments, characters face Dilemmas that force decisions with lasting consequences. These choices directly affect a character's Standing with factions and key characters, alter their reputation, and will grant titles or exposure.

At narrative turning points, the game Master presents a Dilemma Card. Each Card describes a challenging situation and at least two difficult choices. The outcome of each choice is clearly spelled out, affecting rewards, costs, relationships, and a character's standing in the world. Once a decision is made, the GM notes this in their journal and at pivotal moments, and as they choose, the GM narrates the conditions of the choices Players have made for their Characters.

Standing Development

Every choice made on a Dilemma Card adjusts a character's Standing, their relationship and attitude towards factions and important NPCs. Standing is tracked on the Character sheet and determines how others react to them, what resources they can access, and which storylines open or close.

Prestige and Titles

A character's decisions also change their Prestige. Reputation affects how they are perceived throughout the world, influencing social Encounters and opportunities. Titles reflect their achievements or failures and can bring benefits or consequences depending on where they are and how others perceive them.

Note: The highest stance is Cherished while the lowest is Hated (killed on sight).

A character's choices ripple across every aspect of their journey. Decisions influence not just how they are seen, but also their access to resources, titles, relationships with key figures, and the opportunities or obstacles that shape their path. These choices don't just alter the story; they can challenge a Player's priorities, test their values, and create moments that linger long after decisions to Dilemmas are made.

Heroism

Acts of courage and self-sacrifice earn a character recognition and lasting respect. Heroic deeds can grant powerful titles and boost their standing, inspiring others and shaping their legacy.

Regret

Betraying one's values or harming loved ones may cause regret. A character can leverage regret for short-term advantages, but each use comes with emotional or social penalties. Accumulating regret may trigger personal crises or redemption quests.

Suspenseful

Some outcomes are not revealed immediately. The game Master tracks them secretly and unveils the results at dramatic moments, heightening suspense and making a character's choices feel unpredictable and impactful.

Examples

Suspense Example: A character finds proof that a rival faction plans to sabotage a festival. If they expose them, their Standing with that faction drops dramatically, but they gain favor with the city council. The council grants them the title Guardian of the Festival. The rival faction's reaction is kept hidden for now, to be revealed later.

Heroism Example: After quelling a violent uprising and safeguarding countless lives, a character is called to the Grand Hall. The space is packed, council members, citizens, and soldiers line the marble floors. A high ranking council member formally recounts their actions in detail, naming each risk taken and life saved. When their name is spoken, banners are unfurled and the crowd stands and erupts in applause as they walk the marble aisle between arranged sides. As rehearsed, they bow to the council leader so she can place a heavy medallion ribbon over their shoulders and proclaim them "Warden of the City!!!", a title reserved for heroes. Their name is etched into the city's Hall of Heroes, and for a moment, the cheers and gratitude of those they protected are overwhelming. Their Standing with the city's people and leaders surges, and they become a symbol of courage in a time of uncertainty.

Regret Example: An influential leader offers a character Rare resources, but only if they publicly denounce their mentor. Accepting gains them the title Favored of the Court and improves their Standing with the leader's faction, but since they've developed a relationship with their mentor that has lasted for years, the betrayal damages their reputation with those who honor loyalty and loved ones of the mentor in question. This never goes away, and although the example here is an example of a character feeling regret, the dilemma system is designed in a way that the Player feels emotional and psychological distress that weighs on their conscience, burdened from decisions forced onto their characters. Refusing protects the relationship with the mentor, but makes the quest more difficult and may cause the Character to lose favor and titles.

Summary: The Dilemma Engine ensures that a character's choices change their Standing, reputation, and titles, all tracked mechanically. Every tough decision shapes their journey and the world's response to their actions, leaving a lasting mark on their story.

Trials of Combat

Trials of Combat is a game mode where Players seek out Trial Masters to participate in combat challenges. These Masters are found in villages, towns, and covert camps, and they set specific rules for each trial. Participants cannot use items besides gear, though healing and buffing effects are allowed. The mode includes three main sections: Proving Grounds, Arena, and Coliseum. In the Proving Grounds, teams of at least five face objectives like Beacon Capture, King of the Peak, or Race Through the Cave, earning Honor PTS and rewards such as gear and Experience (XP). The Arena involves structured matches from 1v1 up to 5v5, with Players earning Valor PTS to buy equipment, gear sets, and consumables.

The Coliseum is a free-for-all against powerful monsters in various formats like Duels, Tag Teams, and Riots, with winners earning Auctorati PTS for weapons, potions, and scrolls. Progression depends on faction reputation, unlocking better rewards and quests.

Sole Survivor

Sole Survivor is an episodic game mode made up of a series of short, story-driven adventures. At the start of each episode, Players choose pre-made character Cards that match the challenge rating for that episode. These characters form a themed party that fits the campaign, such as a religious character, a thief, or others aligned with the episode's setting. Each premade character will have advantages and disadvantages, but Players will understand the theme before they choose their Cards.

The key rule is that one character must die in each episode, repeated until only one character remains alive. Once only one character is left, they must find a way to escape alone. If they manage to survive and escape, they are declared the sole survivor. Each episode is a short dramatic story where characters will unlock skills, armaments, items and clues and secrets to guide their party through each episode. Episodes don't have endings other than a character dying; subsequently moving what's left of the party to the next episode.

Starting from episode one, the adventure unfolds as a story, with the inevitability that some characters will perish along the way. The game emphasizes survival, storytelling, and strategic choices, culminating in one character's triumphant escape as the sole survivor.

Player

Players are the participants of SORC games that create a character(s) by choosing its race, class, Backgrounds, mission Statements and other various details. The Player also narrates and builds the life of their character(s) throughout their life's journeys, changing the narrative and dynamics of the game's world(s). Players engage with the game's mechanics, collaborate with other Players, and interact with the game world presented to them by the Game Master (GM).

Character

A character is a fictional persona created and controlled by a Player within the TTRPG. Each character typically possesses Unique Traits, Abilities, backgrounds, and motivations that influence their actions and interactions within the game world. Characters can vary widely in nature, ranging from Heroic figures to complex antiheroes, each representing the Player's narrative choices and role within the game. Sometimes the Character is referred to as the Player Character to distinguish between NPC Characters and Player Characters.

Game Master (GM)

The Game Master (abbreviated as GM) is the facilitator of the game's experience, responsible for guiding the narrative and overseeing the game's rules and mechanics. The GM narrates and sometimes even builds the game world, develops the story, portrays the game's Creatures and resolves the outcomes of Player and NPC actions. The GM is a referee and ensures the game remains engaging and balanced, providing exploration challenges and opportunities for Players to build their characters on.

NPC

An NPC (Non-Player Character) is a Character in the game world, not a Player character, but a living part of the world with their own story, role, and purpose. They serve as quest givers, faction leaders, vendors, enemies, and other story-driven figures that bring the world to life. Given their stats, the GM narrates NPC behaviors and rolls dice to determine outcomes.

MAC

MAC (Monsters, Animals, Creatures/Critters) are any living entity in the game world that is not a Player character, beasts, monsters, flora, fauna, and everything in between. They can be hostile, neutral, or friendly, each a living part of the world's ecosystem with their own traits, behaviors, and roles. Given their stats, the GM narrates MAC behaviors and rolls dice to determine outcomes.

Monster

A monster is a hostile or adversarial inhabitant of the game world that creates challenges or threats to Player Characters. Monsters range from traditional fantasy creatures like dragons and ogres to races particularly made for SORC games, such as Boogers (giant frog-like species), Yawnies (sloth-like humanoids) and Pearling (an exotic race evolved from clans millions of years ago). Their characteristics, Abilities, and behaviors are defined by the rules of the game, and their purpose is usually to serve as obstacles for Player Characters to overcome in pursuit of quest objectives. Monsters consist of various hostile beings that serve as adversaries to Players. They embody the antagonistic forces of the game world and are designed to challenge Players with their lives.

Animal

An animal is a living creature that inhabits the game world. Animals can serve various roles such as sources of resources, obstacles, or even allies. They typically possess distinct behaviors and traits, contributing to the richness of the environment and the gameplay experience. Although Animals are often passive, and even friendly, any animals are territorial, defending their space from intruders, and some are protected or endangered, requiring careful interaction. They can also be temperamental, reacting aggressively if threatened or provoked, adding depth and realism to their role within the ecosystem.

Creature

A creature is a broad term that encompasses any living entity within the game world, including both friendly and neutral beings. This includes Critters (bugs and insects), sentient races, animals, magical beings, and even monsters, usually created. Creatures play many roles, including allies, antagonists, both harmful (like mosquitoes) and helpful elements (like edible mushrooms), as well as assist Player Characters in background elements within the narrative and game mechanics. Creatures encompass a wide variety of living beings, often with Unique Abilities or characteristics not limited to sentience. They can be allies or adversaries and play multiple roles in the ecosystem of the game world.

Critter

A Critter is a small, often inconspicuous creature within the game world, such as bugs, insects, small mammals, or tiny magical beings. Despite their size, Critters can have significant roles, serving as pests, sources of resources, or even carriers of special lore or toxins. They are often highly adaptable, elusive, or quick, and may be territorial or skittish, reacting defensively when threatened. Critters enrich the ecosystem, providing Players with opportunities for interaction, collection, or discovery.

Species

A species refers to a distinct group of living beings within the game world, categorized based on shared biological, cultural, or magical characteristics. Each species may possess Unique Traits, Abilities, and cultural significance, shaping their interactions and roles within the game environment. A character can belong to more than one Species at once, Drāco Blood, for instance, are both Humanoid and Reptilian, dragon-blooded humans carrying real draconic ancestry rather than sitting fully in either category alone.

Race

A race is a specific playable lineage within a Species, or a blend of more than one, humans, elves, and dwarves are each their own race within the Humanoid Species, while a hybrid race, like Draco Blood, draws its lineage from more than one Species at once. Race governs a character's Innate Trait Adjustments, Starting ATTR Floor, and Innate Aptitude, see Playable Races, Basic Rules pg. 2.

Divine Species

A Divine species consists of beings that possess extraordinary Abilities and heightened intellect, often exhibiting Traits associated with advanced knowledge, spiritual connection, or Divine influence. These beings might serve as guides, guardians, or powerful allies in the game world. Not to be confused with the Higher Power a Path is bound to (see Basic Rules pg. 2), this is a taxonomic category of being, not the Path mechanic.

Humanoid Species

Humanoid species are those resembling human characteristics in appearance and behavior but may vary in specific ATTRs such as size, skin color, and additional features like horns, claws, or tails. This includes races such as elves, dwarves, and halflings.

Mythical Species

Mythical species are extraordinary beings often rooted in legend, folklore, or ancient tales. They possess unique traits, magical abilities, or divine attributes that set them apart from ordinary creatures and races. These species are typically rare, revered, or feared within the game world, and they often play pivotal roles in the lore, quests, or divine interactions. Their presence can influence the balance of power, inspire awe, or serve as powerful allies or formidable foes.

Beast Species

Any feral Fauna or Monster with a high amount (x or above to be determined) of strength, Toughness and Willpower. Beasts are typically characterized as feral creatures, often possessing heightened physical ATTRs. They can range from powerful predators to wild, untamed animals and can interact with the game world as threats or companions.

Pest Species

Pest species include smaller, often innocuous creatures that populate the game world. These beings can range from insects to tiny mammals and often provide Unique resources or lore. Although Pests are small, many small beings such as spiders, centipedes and other insects carry extremely powerful venoms. Small reptiles and fish, such as; poisonous frogs, venomous snakes, lizards and fish respectively can kill with a single strike. Small animals such as; rats, shrews, lemurs, porcupines, skunks, rabbits, cats, boar, foxes and binturong. Vermin of flight such as; harpy eagles, bats, swifts, squirrels, owls and parrots.

Elemental Species

Elemental species embody the fundamental forces of nature, such as fire, water, earth, and air. They often possess Abilities related to their elemental nature and can shape the environment and gameplay through their powerful elemental connections.

Non-Organic Species

Non-Organic species are constructed or mechanical beings with no biological needs, built rather than born, and powered by an external energy source rather than a metabolism. This includes Droids, Robots, Automatons, Androids, Spacecraft, and Vehicles (hover, water, ground and flight). They are categorized by their power source: Electric, Gas, Photon, Quantum, Steam, Coal, and Magic-powered. A Non-Organic being's power source determines its upkeep, vulnerabilities (such as EMP or water exposure), and what fuel or charge it requires to keep operating.

Extraterrestrial Species

Extraterrestrial species refer to beings from the far side of the known realms, often featuring Unique biological and cultural Traits. These species might introduce new mechanics, Abilities, and narratives, expanding the game world's diversity.

Reptilian Species

Reptilian species are characterized by scaly skin, cold-blooded physiology, and a variety of adaptations to diverse environments. These beings may include lizardfolk, dragons, and other reptilian creatures, often possessing Unique Traits and skills.

Amphibian Species

Amphibian species are creatures that thrive in both aquatic and terrestrial environments, often characterized by moist, smooth skin and the ability to adapt between land and water. They include frogs, salamanders, and newts, and may possess magical or unique traits such as camouflage or toxic skin. Amphibians often play crucial roles in ecosystems as indicators of environmental health and can serve as allies or foes depending on their nature.

Fish Species

Fish species encompass a wide variety of aquatic beings, from small, harmless schooling fish to large, predatory species. They can inhabit lakes, rivers, oceans, and magical waters, often with specialized abilities like bioluminescence, camouflage, or venomous defenses. Fish are vital for the aquatic ecosystem and can be resources for food, ingredients, or even magical components for characters and craft.

Mammal Species

Mammal species include a diverse range of warm-blooded, hairy or fur-covered creatures that inhabit land, air, or water. They range from small rodents to large predators and herbivores, often possessing traits such as intelligence, social behavior, or special senses. Mammals can be domesticated, tamed, or wild, serving roles as companions, mounts, or threats within the game world, and often have complex interactions with the environment and Player characters.

Bird Species

Bird species encompass a wide variety of flying and non-flying avian creatures that inhabit the game world. They range from small, colorful songbirds and scavengers to large raptors like eagles and owls. Birds often possess unique traits such as keen eyesight, flight abilities, or magical properties like communication with spirits or magical signaling. They play vital roles in ecosystems as pollinators, messengers, or predators, and can serve as allies, mounts, or sources of resources for Players depending on their species and behavior.

Insect & Bug Species

Insect and bug species include a vast array of small to medium-sized creatures, ranging from harmless pollinators to deadly predators. Many possess unique adaptations such as venomous stings, chitinous armor, or magical properties imbued in their exoskeletons. These beings often play crucial roles in ecosystems, serving as pollinators, prey, or carriers of potent substances. Some insect species are domesticated or cultivated for resources like silk, venom, or alchemical ingredients, while others are considered pests or mystical entities with special abilities.

Flora and Fauna Species

Flora refers to the plant life present in the game world, including trees, shrubs, flowers, and any other vegetative elements that contribute to the environment. Flora can serve various narrative functions, such as providing resources (like healing herbs), creating obstacles (such as dense thickets), or enhancing the setting's atmosphere. Players may interact with flora in numerous ways, including discovery, harvesting, and even communication and/or combat. Flora encompasses all plant life within the game world, including trees, flowers, herbs, and magical plants. These species can have various uses in gameplay, including crafting, healing, or as obstacles during exploration.

Fauna encompasses the animal life within the game world, including any creatures that are not classified as Creatures or NPCs. This can range from Common wildlife to fantastical creatures Unique to the game setting. Fauna can contribute to the ecosystem of the game world, serve as potential allies or threats, and provide opportunities for Players to engage with their environment. Interactions with fauna provide resources to Player Characters in the game by; hunting, taming, farming and through quests or exploration often unfolding mysteries through the study of various species. Fauna represent the animal life of the game world, ranging from common wildlife to mythical beasts. They contribute to the ecosystem and may provide Players with opportunities for quest-related interactions or resources.

Fae Species

The Fae are an ancient and primordial species, the original inhabitants of Tenue and the ancestral wellspring from which many other races have descended. Connected intrinsically to the magical overgrowth and natural forces that permeate their homeland, the Fae embody a deep affinity for magic and the arcane. Races such as Elves, Firbolgs, and Drow trace their lineage back to the Fae through migrations and transformations, particularly through the mystical pathways of the Veilwood that connect Tenue to Zailister. The Fae possess timeless wisdom, ethereal grace, and a mysterious connection to the very fabric of magic itself, making them potent beings of legend and power within the game world.

Additional Definitions

Class

A class denotes a specific role or archetype that dictates a character's Abilities, strengths, and combat style. Each class comes with a Unique set of skills and ATTRs, influencing how they interact with the game's mechanics, contribute to a party, and face challenges within the game world.

SORC Web

SORC Web is the game's companion online platform, covered in full on Basic Rules pg. 1. It comes in two membership tiers, Basic and Pro, rather than a single version. Basic unlocks for free by downloading the lighter PDF version of the game, and covers community features, forums, digital dice, Tavern Lobbies, Trials of Combat, Community PTS, and server-backed character sheets. Pro unlocks with the Unique code included in every physical box, and includes everything Basic does, plus Private Campaign Rooms, the Essentia Exchange, Character's Home, the SORC Ambiance App, and other Pro-exclusive features. Through SORC Web, Players can also customize and export characters as images or printouts, generate high-resolution Portraits, track a character's Unique ID (UID) for online trading and achievements, recruit a substitute Player when someone misses a session, and log in when a Game Mode, such as Trials of Combat, requires it.

Talent and Profession Bonuses: Races vs. Classes

Favorite Talent Trees and Professions work differently depending on whether they're granted by a Race or a Class.

Classes: A favorite Talent Tree waives the cost to open that tree. A favorite Profession grants a flat bonus to expertise rate.

Abilities

Abilities are Unique combat mechanics or techniques that characters can utilize in battle. Each Ability typically involves a defined effect, ranging from offensive attacks or defensive maneuvers to support measures that bolster allies or hinder foes. Abilities play a crucial role in shaping the outcome of Encounters and influencing strategies.

Aptitudes and Inaptitudes

See Innate Aptitude and Inaptitude, pg. 2, for the full rule, including how they grow as a character ages. Aptitudes and Inaptitudes can absolutely come into play during combat, a Drow's Dark Vision matters in a dark dungeon fight the same as anywhere else, they just aren't Abilities in the formal sense: not part of any class's Tier/Path/Branch tree, no Vitality cost, no weapon requirement, never chosen or activated, always simply on. Every race's specific Aptitudes and Inaptitudes are listed on its own entry (see Playable Races, pg. 2).

Miracles

Miracles are powerful, divinely-granted boons rewarded to characters of Hero rank and above, earned by completing Miracle Quests. A character is not entitled to a Miracle simply for reaching Hero rank, it must be earned through the quest itself. The strength of the Miracle granted is determined by the milestones and achievements completed during that quest, more thorough, difficult, or complete quest resolution yields a stronger Miracle.

Traits

Traits are inherent survival characteristics that a character possesses, granting them advantages in difficult situations or challenges. These Traits can represent resilience, adaptability, or specialized aptitudes, often functioning to mitigate risks or boost performance in specific scenarios within the game world. Traits are a variety of different special actions that include survival, delegation and mitigation. Different than talents that are more or less non combat actions and different than skills which are Profession related so Players have many ways to empower their characters.

Debuff

General Terms

Rule Sets

The three ways a SORC campaign can be run: Bare Bones, Modular and Complex. A Rule Set sets how much of the system is in force, not what any rule says. Chosen before the campaign begins and locked in for its duration. See Rule Sets, pg. 1.

Bare Bones

The bare bones Rule Set. A fixed preset, not a menu: its omissions are set and no Player or GM picks among them. Bare Bones keeps whatever is needed to roll, hit, hurt and advance, and drops the layers that add a lookup, a percentage or a ledger. Alignment is never omitted. Carries its own box set and PDF download. See the Bare Bones page for everything it omits and what a Bare Bones table does instead.

Modular

The Rule Set where the campaign team decides what stays in the rules and what comes out, one feature per category, agreed before the campaign begins and locked in. Modular begins from Complex, every rule in force, and subtracts. It is not a separate box set; a Modular table runs the Complex set and collapses what it dropped.

Complex

The Rule Set with every rule in force, nothing omitted. Carries its own box set and PDF download, and it is the set a Modular table works from. Both the PDF and the online version expand and collapse, so a table can show or hide each system and keep only what they chose in front of them.

Standing

A Character's relationship level with a faction, NPC, or Standing category, on an eight-tier scale carrying numeric values: Exalted +4, Cherished +3, Adored +2, Respected +1, Neutral 0, Tolerable −1, Disliked −2, Hated −3. Held across seven categories, each with its own Standing and Contributions: Family, Affiliations, Companions, Campaign, Fellows, Neighbours, and Wellness. Capitalised Standing always means this; lowercase standing is ordinary English. See Standing under Prestige, pg. 2.

Contribution

What a Character gives to a Standing category, time, Coin, labour, and their own reputation. Contribution and Neglect are one line read in opposite directions, ticking on its own like Wellness. It is the second of the two lines each category carries, the first being Standing, and it does not carry a Mark. Recorded apart from Standing: a Character may contribute heavily and hold poor Standing, or hold high Standing on a name earned long ago.

Neglect

The downward direction of the Contribution line. Neglect can only carry a Character toward Neutral, never past it; falling below Neutral into Tolerable, Disliked, or Hated takes a deliberate act. Absence makes people indifferent, not hostile.

Mark

The highest Standing a Character has ever held with a party, recorded alongside their current Standing. Never decays. Not goodwill still owed but the measure of how far a Character has fallen, and what distinguishes drifting apart from betrayal. A deliberate act drops a party as far below Neutral as their Mark stood above it, minimum one tier. A Bond Quest may seal a Mark, closing it without erasing it.

Praise

What a Character receives from the friends and allies of those they hold good Standing with. Arrives unasked. When Standing with a target moves, allies of that target move the same direction by half the tiers moved, rounded down, minimum 1.

Blowback

What a Character receives from the enemies of those they hold Standing with. Arrives unasked and does not care whether the Character wanted the association. Enemies of a target move opposite the Character's movement with that target, by the same amount as Praise. Rising with one faction is by itself a way of falling with its rivals, so no gain in Standing is neutral.

A race-specific Vitality score representing the volume of each Vitality resource-how much Life, Mana, Stamina, or Endurance a character can hold. Starts at a race-determined value (1-11) and grows every 5 years of age after the first year at a rate specific to that race. Every 5 Apex = 1 Chip of Vitality capacity. Splittable across multiple resources at the Player's choice.

Spirit

A race-specific Vitality score that determines regeneration rate for Life, Mana, or Stamina. Starts at a race-determined value (1-11) and grows every 5 years of age after the first year at a rate specific to that race. Every 5 Spirit = 1 Chip of regeneration per turn. Splittable across Life, Mana, or Stamina at the Player's choice. Never affects Endurance.

Wellness

A percentage (0% to 150%) tracking how well a character takes care of themselves through daily choices, rest, diet, activity, and Practitioner support. Wellness ranges from 0% (minimum, neglect and decline) to 150% (maximum, peak conditioning). Triggered by moral and conscience decisions (healthy choices raise it, unhealthy lower it), poison, disease, and status effects (cause decline), Life loss (hurts Wellness), sustained rest (restores it), practicing abilities (maintains and improves it), healthy diet (strengthens it), and Practitioner visits (provide recovery). Ticks up or down automatically like HoT and DoT effects. Below 75% Wellness, only direct neglect, poison, disease, and injury cause decline; positive effects don't tick. At 75% Wellness and above, both positive and negative effects accelerate (healthy habits snowball faster, bad habits compound quicker). Wellness directly modifies Apex and Spirit: low Wellness shrinks Vitality caps and slows regeneration, high Wellness expands caps and accelerates regeneration. The Trait Health controls tick rate (higher Health slows decline below 75%, speeds recovery at 75% and above). Multiple professions support Wellness: Practitioners, Hunters, Fishermen, Cooks, Scouts, Healers, Buffers, moral leaders, and Field Medics. Rest mechanics: Breather (10 min) ticks slowly, Short Rest (1 hour) ticks faster, Full Rest (8 hours, requires bed) ticks significantly faster; environmental conditions (temperature, comfort, safety) affect rest quality. Characters benefit most from establishing Standing with a primary Practitioner who knows them well. See Wellness, pg. 2, #wellness, and GM Codex Ch. II, #wellness.

Companion Slot Sizes

Characters have four Companion slots, one of each size: Tiny, Small, Standard, and Large/Goliath/Behemoth. The fourth slot is shared and holds either a Large and a Goliath together, or a single Behemoth alone. Dragons are never Companions; drakes, wyverns and other lesser draconic creatures are. Tiny: insects, bugs, and the very smallest creatures. Small: roughly rodent to small bird in size. Standard: humanoid-sized up to the size of a large horse. Large: creatures occupying 2x2 squares. Goliath: large humanoid-scale creatures up to the scale of a Centyr (4 spaces). Behemoth: colossal creatures, extremely rare. Goliath-sized races can mount Large companions or anything larger.

Ranks

Character ranks reflect a hero's reputation and power. NPC and MAC ranks reflect the challenge and tier of any entity encountered. Each tier corresponds directly, a Pauper faces Common-rank threats, a Legend faces Divine. A character must be of a monster or challenge's Rank to engage it, reaching ahead into higher-ranked content isn't hidden, exactly, but blocks off as an obstacle (road guards, sealed gates, terrain too dangerous to cross) until Rank catches up, and none of its items or Companions can be found through exploration either. Characters cannot equip or use items ranked above their own, though they may still carry, store, or gift ones they've legitimately acquired some other way (gifted, traded, inherited). See pg. 4 for full rank rules. A character who retires at Legend rank keeps that status in the world's history, see Retirement, pg. 4.

Creativity, performance, and articulation expressed through precise dexterity, hand-eye coordination, body fluidity, and craftsmanship; encompassing charisma, tactical deception, and the mastery of fine motor skills. Aligns with Dexterity, Deceit, Charisma, Craftsmanship, and Calibration traits.

Knowledge, intuition, and insight. Aligns with Piety and Knowledge traits.

Vitality Summary

Vitality Progression: Life, Mana, Stamina, and Endurance all grow every Character LVL and Growth, plus an aligned Trait bonus for Life/Mana/Stamina. All sizes use the same rates, size identity is reflected in base values. Apex is a race-specific Vitality score starting at a value of 1-11 determined by the character's race, increasing whichever Vitality resource(s) a character allocates it to (Player's choice, splittable across resources). Apex grows every 5 years of age after the first year at a rate specific to the character's race (every 5 Apex = 1 Chip of capacity). Spirit is a race-specific Vitality score starting at a value of 1-11 determined by the character's race, providing regeneration from whichever of Life, Mana, and Stamina a character allocates it to (Player's choice, splittable, every turn, always active). Spirit grows every 5 years of age after the first year at a rate specific to the character's race (every 5 Spirit = 1 Chip of regeneration per turn). Neither Apex nor Spirit are purchased with any currency; both are their own Vitality scores, not Traits, and grow automatically through age and Growth progression. Nature Abilities cost Stamina, Spell Abilities cost Mana, both scale with the LVL learned. Endurance base: 35 for all sizes, resets daily.

Base Vitality by Size: Goliath, Life 60, Mana 24, Stamina 40, Endurance 35. Standard, Life 50, Mana 32, Stamina 32, Endurance 35. Small, Life 40, Mana 40, Stamina 24, Endurance 35.

Armament Penalty (Armor) and Weapon/Hand Proficiency (Weapons)

A character may use ANY class's Abilities, from any Class Tree they've spent Ability points into, with no Armament Penalty and the full Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus, so long as two things are true that day: the class's own default Armaments (its normal armor and weapon types) are part of that day's Armament Set, and they are physically able to carry what's equipped. Abilities already learned remain fully usable even if the character can no longer access that class's Path due to alignment or Fallen/Risen mechanics.

Armor and weapons are not treated the same way when mismatched. Armor is a matter of weight and mobility, something raw physical capability can overcome, wearing heavier than a tree allows costs a scaling Armament Penalty. A weapon is a matter of Culture instead, and no Trait overcomes it: a tree's Abilities require one of that tree's listed weapons to be readied, and a weapon it does not list, or one it names Forbidden, performs none of them. Wielding the tree's own stated weapon both satisfies that requirement and grants its Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus.

Armor Grade Threshold: Every class, Path, and Branch has a maximum Armor Grade its Abilities assume, stated in that tree's Armaments reminder (see Playable Classes, pg. 3, and Armor Grades & Materials, pg. 4, for the eleven Grades in ascending order: Delicate, Half Light, Light, Half Medium, Medium, Half Heavy, Heavy, Half Dense, Dense, Half Composite, Composite). Each Half Grade sits between a full Grade and the full Grade below it, a blend of the two, Half Light blends Light and Delicate, Half Medium blends Medium and Light, Half Heavy blends Heavy and Medium, Half Dense blends Dense and Heavy, Half Composite blends Composite and Dense. A character may wear their tree's maximum Grade, or any lighter Grade, with no Armament Penalty at all, weight and mobility below that ceiling are never a Prerequisite problem.

Armor Mismatch, Armament Penalty: When a character uses an Ability from a tree while wearing an Armor Grade heavier than that tree's maximum allows, a -5 Armament Penalty applies per Grade of difference, two Grades over the threshold applies -10, three Grades over applies -15, and so on. This penalty hits every roll made using that specific Ability, whether it's an attack roll, a Skill check, a crafting check, or any other action the Ability calls for, but only while that mismatched Ability is actually being used, never to unrelated actions. The penalty reduces by 1 per Grade of difference for every +5 a character has in whichever Trait most directly relates to overcoming that mismatch, typically Strength for encumbering armor. The GM determines the relevant Trait when it isn't obvious.

Example: A Monk's tree assumes Delicate Grade or lighter. A character with Heavy Grade (Plate) donned as their daily Armament is six Grades over that threshold (Delicate → Half Light → Light → Half Medium → Medium → Half Heavy → Heavy), taking a -30 Armament Penalty using any Monk Nature Ability. With +5 Strength, that penalty reduces to -24.

Protector Trees: A handful of Paths and Branches built around frontline defense raise their tree's maximum Grade all the way to Dense or Composite, the two heaviest Grades that exist (see Armor Grades & Materials, pg. 4), meaning a character following one of these can don ANY armor in the game with zero Armament Penalty. This is never free: Dense and Composite both carry the steepest movement penalties of any Grade, and every Ability written for a Protector Path or Branch is built to trade damage output for raw survivability, expect a Protector to hit softer and move slower than a peer who stayed in lighter armor. See each class's Tiers, Paths and Branches, pg. 3, for which ones carry this trait.

Weapon/Hand Proficiency: Every class, Path, and Branch has an ideal weapon profile stated in that tree's Armaments reminder (see Playable Classes, pg. 3), covering five parts: a Weapon Type (Tactical, Magic, TAW, or Chthonic, see Weapon Type DMG Mods, pg. 4), a Weapon Style (Slashing, Blunt, Piercing, or Chop, for melee weapons; Range and Throw weapons carry no Style, see below), a Hand (1h (Main), 1h (Off), or 2h), a Distance (Touch, Melee, Throw, or Range, each with its own distance band, see below), and a Grade & Material (the weapon's Grade, see Weapon Grade & Materials, pg. 4, and, for some Paths or Branches, a Material Restriction that narrows the Weapon Style further, such as wood-only). A weapon must satisfy every part of a tree's profile to earn that tree's full Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus, matching the Weapon Type alone is not enough if the Style, Hand, Distance, or Grade & Material don't also match. A weapon that satisfies the tree's Weapon Type and Style, without being Forbidden by its Culture, is enough to perform the tree's Abilities at all; the remaining parts govern only the bonus.

Range: Every weapon and Spell resolves at one of five Ranges: Touch (self or an adjacent target, no distance), Melee (adjacent, weapon's reach), Throw (a thrown weapon's short distance, e.g. javelin, hand-axe, sling stone), Area (Spell-only, a distance shorter than Range that resolves against a zone), or Range (a mechanically launched weapon's long distance, e.g. bow, crossbow, black powder weapon, or a Spell's longest single-target cast distance). Throw and Range are not interchangeable, a thrown weapon and a drawn/mechanically loosed weapon are different Ranges even when their real-world distances overlap. Area still carries any Style (Cone, Line, Burst, Cloud, Single Target, or Chain), the Style just plays out at Area's shorter distance, a Line Style at Area Range becomes several short rays fanning out rather than one long beam, a Cone Style at Area Range becomes a tighter, closer wedge rather than a long one.

Weapon Type vs. Weapon Style: A weapon's Type is Tactical (ordinary craft), Magic (Enchanted, or a caster's implement by nature), TAW (manufactured technology, splitting into TAW Ballistic and TAW Charged), or Chthonic (elemental substance held in the shape of a weapon, melee or ranged, neither cast nor built, and equally strong on every world, see Chthonic Weapons, pg. 4), the same Martial-or-Magic split used to flavor-tag Slots on pg. 3, plus the two culture-gated Types. A weapon's Style, where it has one, is Slashing, Blunt, Piercing, Chop, Flexible, Grab, or Chain, describing a melee weapon's shape, not its Type. The two combine: Tactical Blunt covers hammers, maces, clubs, and Staffs (Quarterstaff, Bō, Jō, Long Staff (2h), Short Staff (1h, dual-wieldable)); Magic Blunt covers Staves and wands, the dedicated caster implement, never a martial Staff, distinct in name specifically to avoid confusion between the two. The same split carries across the other Styles: a plain blade is Tactical Slashing, an enchanted blade is Magic Slashing; a plain dagger or spear is Tactical Piercing, an enchanted one is Magic Piercing; a plain axe or hatchet is Tactical Chop, an enchanted axe is Magic Chop; a plain rope or whip is Tactical Flexible, an enchanted one is Magic Flexible; a plain pair of tongs, pincers, claws, or hooks is Tactical Grab, an enchanted pair is Magic Grab. Unlike every other Style, Grab never rolls standard attack damage, its modifier applies to a Grapple or Grab check instead (see Grapple and Grab, Basic Rules pg. 5). Chain stands apart from the rest, it isn't forged into one fixed shape, a chain-whip is Flexible, a chain-sword is Slashing, and a chain-and-weight mounted on a pole is Blunt, each specific Chain weapon states which of the three it counts as on its own Card, using that row's DMG mod rather than a row of its own. Throw and Range weapons carry no Style at all, a javelin or hand-axe is Tactical (Range: Throw); a bow or black powder weapon is Tactical (Range: Range); a wand or orb channeled at range is Magic (Range: Range). Not every specific weapon crosses both Types, some are locked to one side by their nature, a two-handed hammer can only ever be Tactical, an orb can only ever be Magic, while others, like blades, can go either way depending on how they're made. For damage-modifier purposes (see Weapon Type DMG Mods, pg. 4), a Magic-Type weapon uses the Magic row regardless of Style, a Tactical-Type weapon uses its Style's own row instead. TAW and Chthonic stand apart from both Type values and from each other, both gated by culture and availability rather than training or magic (see Cultural Armaments & Rarity, below), but they are not the same thing: TAW is manufactured technology, a TAW blade or rifle built on an assembly line; Chthonic is elemental substance given a weapon's shape, a blade of living flame, a lightning whip, a coiled wind-blade, plasma, or raw force, never manufactured, mirroring the same TABA-versus-Chthonic split defined for armor above.

Spell Prerequisite Profile: Spells carry the same kind of profile weapons do, but built from five Spell-specific parts instead: a Cast (Verbal, a spoken incantation or chant; Somatic, a gesture or hand motion; Ritual, no Verbal or Somatic component required, but takes extended time to perform; Mind, no spoken, gestured, or ritual component at all, cast through will alone, covering ESP, psionics, and telekinesis; Reagents, cast by consuming a Reagent or Organic Element (Bone Dust, Bat Wings, Elderwood Ash, and the like, see Organic Elements, pg. 4) in place of Mana; Conjure, calls forth or animates a weapon, construct, or remains into being or motion, such as a conjured blade, spectral shield, or reanimated bones, that lingers and acts on its own each turn until dismissed or the encounter ends, not interruptible and not requiring further action from the caster, who is free to act or perform skill checks without losing it; Summon, calls a living or spirit creature to act with limited agency on the caster's behalf, such as a bound spirit-hawk or a hovering shrunken head, under the same non-interruptible terms as Conjure; or Orbital, an entity of various forms, shaped from raw magic rather than a weapon, creature, or remains, that hovers or floats around the caster to protect, attack, buff, or curse on its own, typically lasting for longer periods of time rather than resolving in a single instant, under the same non-interruptible terms as Conjure), a Mechanic (Instant, resolves immediately; DoT, damage ticking each round; HoT, healing ticking each round; Channel, held up turn after turn and interruptible if the caster is struck; or Drain, cast by spending Life, Stamina, or Endurance instead of Mana), a Style (Cone, a wedge extending from the caster; all other Styles, such as Line, Burst, Cloud, Single Target, and Chain, TBA), a School (the Spell's elemental or magical flavor, e.g. Fire, Ice, Arcane, Acid, Lightning, Necrotic, Holy, Psychic, Nature, Psionic; a School can carry its own lingering sub-effect, such as Acid's Corrosion degrading equipment over time; Psionic further splits into named disciplines, Telekinesis, Clairvoyance, Metacreativity, and Psychometabolism, alongside unfocused Psionic itself, see Imperials, Basic Rules pg. 2), and a Distance (Touch, Range, or Area, see above; a Spell channeled through a melee or thrown weapon instead uses that weapon's Melee or Throw Range). A Spell's full profile is noted on its Spell Card.

Armor Type: Armor splits four ways, Tactical (ordinary craft, steel, leather, hide, bone), Magic (its protective quality comes from an inherent magical nature rather than craft alone), TABA (Technologically Advanced Body Armor, gated by culture and availability the same as TAW, see pg. 4), and Chthonic (elemental in nature, plasma, living flame, raw force rather than manufactured plating, gated the same way as TABA but never built by Omnè's engineering). TABA and Chthonic are not the same thing, TABA is manufactured technology, Chthonic is elemental substance given form, a plasma cuirass is Chthonic even if no factory anywhere produced it. Any Armor Grade can in principle be reached by any of the four Types, a Heavy Grade breastplate might be plain Tactical steel, TABA plating, elemental Chthonic given shape, or a rare Magic-forged piece, though some Structures lean toward one Type by nature, Force Fields are inherently TABA, Chthonic Shields are inherently Chthonic, and Mithril is inherently Magic, Type, Structure, and Grade (Half Light) are one and the same for it, it is never built as Tactical, TABA, or Chthonic.

Cultural Armaments & Rarity: TAW and Chthonic are not special cases, they're an example of a wider rule: every playable race has its own culture-tied Armaments, a Weapon Type, armor style, or Tactical or Magic technique unique to its people. Troll-make weaponry and armor and Ignis's Primal Armaments and Accessories (see pg. 4) are only two named examples among many, every race carries its own, whether written up yet or not. Each is rare outside that culture's reach. It becomes common only when at least one of two things is true: the campaign setting is within or near that culture's territory (an Omnè refugee camp counts, even off-world, same for a settlement on or near Ignis), or the Character themselves belongs to that culture, whether by race or by a Background raised among its people (see Backgrounds, pg. 2), a human raised by Omnè qualifies the same as an Omnè character would, same for one raised on Ignis. Outside both conditions, that culture's Armaments are rare to find, buy, or craft, though a GM may still roll to let a character have managed to acquire a piece anyway, at their discretion. Many interactive MAC creatures carry their own MAC-exclusive Armaments the same way, entire classes built around them, Grave Wardens (Undead) wield Deathrattle bone weaponry and wrap themselves in Shroud Plate cured from grave-cloth and bone; Core-Wrought Sentinels (Construct) integrate a Forged-Core weapon limb and Plated Chassis armor directly into their bodies rather than wearing or wielding either; Alpha Predators (Beast) fight with natural Feral Claw and Hidebound weapon and armor types no character can equip or remove. These, and many more, are detailed per Module and/or in Behemoths, Beasts & Bugs rather than here.

Required Weapons: Every Tier, Path, and Branch lists the weapons its Abilities are performed with. One of them must be readied to use any Ability from that tree. This is not a penalty scale and no Trait softens it: a weapon the tree does not list, or one its Culture names Forbidden, simply performs none of that tree's Abilities. A Character may still carry and fight with whatever they like, Basic ATTKs are never restricted by class, Path, or Branch, and any weapon a Character can wield performs one normally (see Basic ATTKs, Basic Rules pg. 5). The restriction exists for Culture rather than capability: an Avenger is perfectly able to swing a TAW mace, their faith simply does not channel through manufactured tech, so no Avenger Ability comes out of it.

Weapon Match, Weapon/Hand Proficiency: Satisfying a tree's requirement and earning its bonus are two different bars. Wielding the weapon that satisfies a tree's full profile grants that tree's stated Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus, typically a flat bonus to Hit. Where a Path or Branch calls out a specific alternate weapon of its own, wielding that one instead grants a smaller version of the same bonus. This bonus is separate from the Armament Penalty above, and unlike armor, no amount of Strength, Agility, or any other Trait changes either number, they're fixed by what's in hand.

Example: An Avenger's Abilities assume a Blunt, 1h or 2h, Melee weapon, wielding one grants the Avenger's Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus. An Avenger wielding a dagger instead still uses every Avenger Ability normally, they simply don't earn that bonus until a Blunt weapon is back in the day's Armament Set. A Sorcerer channels through a Magic, 1h or 2h, Melee-range Stave for the same bonus; wielding a different melee weapon instead still casts every Sorcerer Spell normally, just without it.

Example, Material Restriction: A Monk's tree calls for a Blunt weapon made of wood, 1h, 2h, or bare hands, Melee range, to earn its Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus. A hammer or mace is Blunt and Melee, but its metal head fails the wood-only Material Restriction, so it doesn't earn the bonus, though it still activates every Monk Nature Ability normally. A quarterstaff or nunchaku, being both Blunt and wood, satisfies the full profile and earns the bonus.

Using a class's Abilities while equipped with that SAME class's own default Armaments for the day carries no Armament Penalty and earns that tree's full Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus, no matter how another class's Armaments compare.

Enchantment Creates a Magic Weapon: A weapon's Magic Type is not inherent in every case, most weapons start as Tactical, and it's a successful Enchantment (see Enchanting, pg. 4) that converts a Tactical weapon into a Magic one, this is what defines a magic weapon in these rules, not a glowing effect or a magical-sounding name, but having actually undergone Enchantment. A handful of dedicated caster implements, a Stave, wand, orb, or tome, are Magic by their own nature instead (see Weapon Type vs. Weapon Style, above) and never need Enchanting to qualify, the same way an orb can never be Tactical no matter what's done to it.

Magic Weapons, Martial, and Spell: A Magic-Type weapon earns both a Martial Ability's and a Spell Ability's Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus at once, so long as its Style, Hand, Distance, and Grade & Material still match the tree's ideal profile, while a Tactical-Type weapon only earns it for a Martial Ability. This means a class like Barbarian, Ranger, Warrior, or Rogue, whose tree's Weapon/Hand Proficiency profile stays weapon-based rather than Stave-or-orb-based across Tier, Path, and Branch, still performs its Martial Abilities with the plain Tactical version of that profile, the same as always, but casting one of its Spell Abilities at full bonus calls for either an Enchanted (Magic-Type) version of that same weapon, or the tree's specific default casting implement where one is stated instead (a Stave, orb, tome, wand, or similar), if that tree defines one as its true baseline focus, either way the Spell itself still casts without one. For example, a Barbarian's 2h axe, once successfully Enchanted (see Enchantment Creates a Magic Weapon, above), becomes a Magic-Type 2h axe that earns the Barbarian's Spell Weapon/Hand Proficiency bonus the same way a caster's Stave or orb would, no separate implement needed, so long as its Style, Hand, Distance, and Grade & Material still match the Barbarian's own ideal profile.

Example, Martial In Hand, Casting Without One: A Salii Priest can strike with a plain dagger as a Martial Ability normally, no Mana spent and no Enchantment needed, that half of the tree never touches either. To cast one of Salii's Spell Abilities through that same dagger instead, the dagger must first be Enchanted into a Magic-Type dagger, plain Tactical steel doesn't channel a Spell no matter how it's swung. An Avenger Cleric works the same way with a hammer: swinging it as a Martial Ability costs no Mana and needs no Enchantment, wielded empty-handed of magic entirely if that's all the Cleric has on them, but casting a Spell Ability through that hammer calls for both at once, enough Mana in the tank to pay the Spell's cost, and a Magic-Type hammer to actually channel it, a Tactical hammer and an empty Mana pool each block it on their own.

Hybrid Abilities and Reagents: Reagent cost is not tied to which class an Ability belongs to, Caster-tree or Martial-tree, it's tied to the Ability itself. Standing Categories

Standing is held across seven categories, each with its own Standing and its own Contributions, tracked apart: Family (the Character's kin), Affiliations (formal faction membership), Companions (Pets, Guardians, mounts, Fellowships, recorded as Mood), Campaign (people partied with), Fellows (the friends list), Neighbours (those sharing or bordering the Character's coordinates), and Wellness (the Character's Standing with themselves, their self-directed Contributions). See Standing under Prestige for full descriptions.

Contribution, Neglect & the Mark

Each Standing category carries two lines: Standing (Exalted through Hated, how the party regards the Character) and Contribution (what the Character puts in, with Neglect as its downward direction, ticking on its own like Wellness). Recorded apart. Only the Standing line carries a Mark, so each category holds three numbers: Standing now, the Mark, and Contribution now.

Neglect floor: Neglect can only carry a Character toward Neutral, never past it. Falling below Neutral takes a deliberate act.

The Mark: the highest Standing ever held with a party, recorded alongside current Standing. Never decays. It distinguishes drifting apart from betrayal: two parties at Neutral read differently if one's Mark is Exalted and the other's is Neutral.

Betrayal scales with the Mark: a deliberate act drops a party as far below Neutral as their Mark stood above it. Mark Adored (+2) lands at Disliked (−2); Mark Exalted (+4) lands at Hated. Minimum one tier below Neutral regardless of Mark.

Sealing: a Mark is never erased, but a Bond Quest may seal it. The record stands; the GM stops applying it. See Contribution, Neglect & the Mark under Prestige, pg. 2.

Praise & Blowback

Praise is what a Character receives from the friends and allies of those they hold good Standing with. Blowback is what they receive from the enemies of those same parties. Both arrive unasked. Rising with one faction is by itself a way of falling with its rivals, so there is no neutral gain in Standing. A mission statement worked towards draws Praise from those who hope to see it done and Blowback from those who do not; a mission statement neglected reverses both. The ripple: when Standing with a target moves, allies of that target move the same direction by half the tiers moved, rounded down, minimum 1; enemies move the opposite direction by the same amount; parties marked neutral do not move. See Praise & Blowback under Prestige, pg. 2.

Non-Player Character Disposition

NPCs interact based on their Standing relative to the Character, their race, and their faction. Some Standings are set by default (racial defaults), and shift through Player actions and story events. Standing runs: Exalted, Cherished, Adored, Respected, Neutral, Tolerable, Disliked, Hated. Exalted is reached only through a Bond Quest, which becomes available at Cherished. Titles: Glory, Shame, and Reputation

Titles are the world's memory of a character. They represent deeds done, accomplishments earned, or infractions committed. Titles are earned or imposed, displayed or hidden, and affect how NPCs and factions perceive and interact with the character. A title is more than flavor; it shapes the game world's response to who the character is and what they have done.

Boasted Titles: Earned Glory

Boasted Titles are prestigious honors earned through heroic deeds, achievements, or distinguished actions. A player may BOAST one title at a time, displaying it in their Hero slot for the world to see. Boasted titles grant Standing bonuses with factions that recognize and respect them, and they feed directly into a character's Aura (see Aura, pg. 4).

How Boasted Titles are Earned: Titles are earned through:

  • Completing major quests or missions that impact the world.
  • Defeating legendary enemies or accomplishing impossible feats.
  • Achieving a new Rank tier (each Rank tier met is an achievement that can be boasted).
  • Earning recognition from major factions or NPCs.
  • Accumulating Prestige and Standing in specific directions (becoming a Hero, a Sage, a Champion, etc.).

Tracking Boasted Titles: A character may earn many Boasted Titles throughout their career, but only one is actively displayed at a time. The player chooses which title to boast, changing it as their character's focus shifts. All earned titles remain in the character's permanent record (Journal booklet or Space), providing a history of accomplishments. If a player forgets or fails to track earned titles, they are lost; maintaining careful records is rewarded.

Shamed Titles: The Mark of Infamy

Shamed Titles are marks of disgrace, infamy, or moral failing. They are issued by the GM as consequences for significant negative deeds, exposure of secrets, or betrayals. Unlike Boasted Titles, a character does not choose or consent to Shamed Titles; they are imposed as consequences. Shamed Titles are always EXPOSED, visible to the world, and permanently recorded.

How Shamed Titles are Issued: A GM issues a Shamed Title when:

  • A character's secret deed is discovered through Witness Probability rolls (see Witness Probability, below).
  • A character commits a significant act of betrayal, cruelty, or moral failing within the game world.
  • A character's actions trigger NPC or faction judgment (a Companion abandoner, a murderer, a Heretic, etc.).
  • The story or module specifically grants a Shamed Title as a consequence.

Mechanics of Shamed Titles: When a Shamed Title is issued:

  • It is automatically EXPOSED and visible to the world; characters cannot hide it during the locking period.
  • It causes Standing loss with factions that oppose or judge the deed.
  • It triggers Blowback from those factions' allies and enemies (see Praise & Blowback, above).
  • It affects how NPCs respond to the character; some will refuse service, attack on sight, or demand compensation.
  • It feeds directly into the character's Aura, making them appear darker, corrupted, or broken (see Aura, pg. 4).

Title Locking and Interchanging

Lock Duration: When a title (Shamed or Boasted) is first issued or earned, it is LOCKED for a minimum of one GAME DAY (one full rest). During this period, a Shamed Title cannot be hidden, and a Boasted Title cannot be swapped for another.

Visibility Increases Over Time: The longer a title remains active and exposed, the more people see it, speak of it, and build their opinions around it. A fresh Shamed Title "Murderer" might be unknown to distant factions, but after weeks of in-game time, NPCs and factions across the realm have heard whispers. Standing ripples spread faster and farther the longer a title is visible. Conversely, a Boasted Title gains more power the longer it remains visible, granting bonuses that compound over time.

Interchanging After Lock Expires: Once the one-day lock expires, a character who has earned multiple Shamed or Boasted titles may interchange between them. Only one Shamed Title and one Boasted Title may be actively displayed at any given time. Switching titles requires a change of circumstances or deliberate action, and the GM may allow or deny the interchange based on narrative logic. A character cannot simply swap titles daily; each swap should reflect meaningful story change (retiring a title, embracing a new identity, the world forgetting an old one).

GM Tracking of Shamed Titles: The GM maintains a master record of all Shamed Titles in the character's Journal booklet. This record is PERMANENT. Even interchanged or hidden titles remain in the archive, and NPCs with long memories may recall them. The GM controls how long a Shamed Title remains EXPOSED to the world; this duration depends on story pacing, faction responses, and the character's actions in response to the title. The GM may also choose whether a newly discovered deed issues a new Shamed Title that supersedes an existing one, or stacks alongside it. Shamed Titles dissipate only through redemption quests, years of good behavior, or specific rituals tied to their nature (see Common Shamed Titles, below).

Stacking of Titles: A character may have multiple active (though hidden) titles at once. The GM may stack new Shamed Titles on an existing character, creating layers of infamy. Players may discover that forgotten titles still haunt them or that NPCs recall older deeds. This is intentional; the past does not disappear.

Witness Probability: How Deeds Become Exposed

Not all deeds are seen. When a character commits a significant act in secret: murder in a desolate location, theft from a guarded vault, betrayal in a private meeting, the GM rolls to determine if witnesses are present. The baseline probability depends on the location, time, and circumstances. A character may commit an act in near-total secrecy; success is never guaranteed, but persistence raises the odds.

Scenarios and Modifiers: Witness probability varies by scenario: desolate areas have low baseline odds, populated areas have high baseline odds, repeat offenses in the same location increase odds with each occurrence. Time of day, presence of magical creatures or wisps, and character actions (leaving evidence, fleeing conspicuously) all modify the GM's roll. See Witness Probability Formula (GM-Codex) for the complete mathematical framework and scenario modifiers.

Cumulative Probability: The first time a character commits an act in a location, they may succeed in hiding it. The second time in the same location, witnesses become more likely. By the third or fourth repeat, discovery becomes nearly inevitable. The world grows smaller, and secrets grow cheaper. Each incident compounds; the GM tracks this privately.

Exposure and Shamed Titles: When a deed is discovered (witnessed or revealed), the GM may issue a Shamed Title befitting the deed. The severity and type of Title depend on what was done and how it impacts the world around the character. A character who commits murder may receive "Murderer"; a character who abandons an ally receives "Turncoat" or worse. The character does not choose or consent to Shamed Titles; they are imposed by the GM as a consequence of exposure.

Class Trainer Requirements & Fees

A Class Trainer needs to teach the Character's Main Class, not the exact Tier, Path, or Branch, any Trainer who teaches that Main Class can teach any Tier, Path, or Branch within it (see Playable Classes, pg. 3). Paths and Branches are Sub-Classes of their Main Class, not separate trees of their own, freely chosen at LVL 4 and LVL 21 respectively. Separately, certain Slots carry a Prerequisite: every 4th LVL starting at LVL 3, one Slot, cycling A, B, C, is a Prerequisite for that same Slot and Split at the very next LVL (see Slots and Prerequisites, pg. 3). A Trainer must be at least the Character's own current LVL, this holds whether the pick being learned came from reaching that LVL outright or from the midpoint toward the next one, the requirement tracks the Character's LVL, not which Slot the pick filled. Reaching a LVL, or the midpoint toward the next one, that grants a pick only makes it available, the pick still isn't learned until the Character finds a qualifying Trainer, in sufficient faction standing, and pays the fee.

Slot RangeSuggested Fee (Gold)
Tier (1-3)5 × Slot Number
Path (4-20)10 × Slot Number
Branch (21-30)20 × Slot Number

Example: A character's 13th pick, a Path Slot, costs a suggested 130 Gold (10 × 13), learned from any Trainer of that Main Class who is at least the Character's own current LVL. A character's 27th pick, a Branch Slot, costs a suggested 540 Gold (20 × 27), under the same Trainer requirement.

These are suggested baseline fees, not fixed prices. Each Module sets its own actual Class Trainer fees and Trainer availability, the same way Planetary Markets pricing varies by world and region (see pg. 4). A GM may adjust a given Trainer's fee upward for a rare specialist, or downward for a common local one.

Fallen & Risen

Scope: Only Paths bound to a Higher Power can Fall or Rise; Ambivalent Paths cannot (they have no patron to betray or appease).

Which Paths Can Fall or Rise: Lawful and Moral Paths may Fall. Amoral and Evil Paths may Rise. (Alignment is set at character creation; a character's choices during play can shift their alignment, which in turn may trigger Fallen or Risen mechanics for a bound Path.)

Fallen Mechanic: When a Lawful or Moral Path's character neglects devotion (missing daily Endurance actions or weekly ceremonies for extended periods), the Higher Power tests for Fallen: the character loses all Higher Power bonuses tied to that Path and its Abilities, and begins taking penalties and random tests from the wronged patron. The character keeps every Ability already learned from that Path but can no longer learn new Abilities from it until the bond is restored. Neglect continues until the debt is repaid through renewed devotion and ceremony (see Recovery and Redemption, below).

Risen Mechanic: When an Amoral or Evil Path's character betrays their patron's core values (refusing services, breaking oaths, or working against the patron's interests for extended periods), the Higher Power tests for Risen: the character loses all Higher Power bonuses tied to that Path and its Abilities, and begins taking penalties and wrath from the betrayed patron. The character keeps every Ability already learned from that Path but can no longer learn new Abilities from it until the bond is restored. Betrayal continues until the debt is repaid through service to the patron or finding a new Higher Power (see Recovery and Redemption, below).

Recovery and Redemption: A broken bond may be restored by returning to full devotion (Lawful/Moral) or renewed service (Amoral/Evil) until the patron deems the debt repaid, typically after 4-6 weeks of resumed ceremony and service. The Higher Power may test the character's sincerity. Once restored, the character regains all bonuses and may once more learn new Abilities from that Path. Alternatively, a character may petition a different Higher Power at LVL 5 or beyond (abandoning the broken Path's patron entirely), but this choice is permanent and irrevocable, the old patron's wrath remains unless the new patron offers reparations or the debt is paid in full.

Professions & Social Roles

Professions represent specialized expertise and social roles characters develop beyond their combat abilities. The game recognizes two profession frameworks:

  • Character Professions: Specialized skills available to player characters, organized by four categories: Crafting, Building, Automation, and Exploration. Each Character Profession pairs with a corresponding Talent Tree for synergistic development. See Character Professions.
  • NPC Professions: All social roles available to non-player characters, including those overlapping with Character Professions. Organized into 13 categories spanning Commerce, Justice, Technology, Mysticism, and more. See NPC Professions.

Character Profession Synergy: Each Talent Tree contains a Profession bonus talent granting benefits to its paired Profession. Investing in both a Talent Tree and its paired Profession creates synergy, the combined advancement yields greater returns than developing either alone. Professional Skills advance through practice and Proficiency checks within the profession's domain.

NPC Profession Flexibility: NPCs may pursue any profession without the Talent Tree requirement, allowing greater flexibility in role assignment and social positioning within the game world. An NPC Innkeeper, Tax Collector, or Judge operates with full professional authority regardless of combat capability.
