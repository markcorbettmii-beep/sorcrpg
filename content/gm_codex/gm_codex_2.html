Chapter II: Character Progression

Basic Rules pg. 2 introduces Growth, Character Level, and Rank as a summary. This chapter is the full, unabridged version of all three, the tables, schedules, and procedures a GM needs to run progression at the table.

Growth Development

Growth is a character's maturity and aging, measured and recorded in Eons of Adoria (EA), or "years."

A character ages every year they're played, but Growth itself doesn't arrive every year, the wait grows each time. The first Growth takes 1 year. The second takes 2 more years after that. The third takes 3 more after that, and so on, each Growth requiring one additional year over the last (see How a Year Passes, below, for what counts toward that wait).

Every Growth grants Vitality (Life, Mana, Stamina, Endurance) increases, see the table below.

A character's age always starts at their race's Starting Age and climbs every year they're played, whether or not a Growth lands that year, they progress in Vitality only when a Growth actually arrives, as shown in the table above.

Various races have different starting ages within their 40 year retirement limit (see each race's entry, Basic Rules pg. 2) and the Lifespan, Starting Age, and Retirement Age table in the Basic Rules pg. 6 for every race's numbers.

Retirement arrives automatically at year 40, the same window for every race no matter how differently their story started (see Retirement, Basic Rules pg. 4) or their current rank. Because Growth's wait grows every time, Growth 8 (36 cumulative years) is about as far as any character will get before that window closes, Growth 9 and beyond exist for reference and extended-play campaigns, but sit past what a standard 40-year career reaches.

Drawn Abilities

Unlike Vitality, Drawn Ability picks don't wait for Growth's escalating schedule. A character earns exactly one Drawn Ability pick per year of age lived, every year, flat, no escalation and no skipping. Over a standard 40-year career, that's up to 40 total picks available across a lifetime (see Drawn Abilities, Basic Rules pg. 7, for what a pick actually is, Nature Drawn or Spell Drawn, player's choice each time). Because Retirement still arrives at year 40 for every race regardless of Drawn Ability picks, a character who spends every year on a pick reaches the full 40, unlike Growth, nothing about this pace caps out early.

Each Drawn Ability tree, Nature Drawn or Spell Drawn, and multiple named trees exist within each, holds 30 abilities, and a character isn't locked into a single tree; picks can be spent across as many trees as they like. But only one tree, ever, may reach its 20th ability, the capstone. A second tree a character invests heavily in can climb as high as its 19th ability, one short of its own capstone, or picks can be divided more broadly across additional trees for diversity instead. A character who commits to one 20-ability capstone tree and one 19-ability runner-up tree has spent 39 of their 40 lifetime picks, leaving exactly 1 for anything else.

Abilities within a tree grow in power as a character climbs deeper into it, not evenly, each ability further in is a meaningful jump over the last. The 20th ability, a tree's capstone, is an extremely powerful once-per-day ability worth building an entire career around.

Drawn Ability Distribution Examples

Example A: Maxed Capstone

A character dedicates their career to raw power: one tree, whichever the player chooses, pushed all the way to its 20th ability, the single most powerful choice available in any Drawn Ability tree, and a second tree pushed to its 19th, developing as much power in it as the one-capstone rule allows. 20 + 19 = 39, leaving exactly 1 of the 40 lifetime picks for anything else.

Example B: Middle Ground

A character deliberately chooses a middle-ground set instead of chasing a capstone: three trees built to a solid, balanced depth of 15 + 15 + 10 = 40. No tree reaches its 20th ability, but all three carry real weight in play.

Example C: Maximum Diversity

A character goes as diverse as the system allows: 40 different trees, one ability picked from each. No tree gets anywhere close to deep, but the character can call on a wider spread of Nature Drawn and Spell Drawn Abilities than any other build in the game.

How a Year Passes

A single year is 1 Eon of Adoria, 360 days (see Time, Basic Rules pg. 4), built from the same units as everything else on the clock: the 6-second Turn and the Round (every character's Turn taken once, see Turns, Rounds and Time, Basic Rules pg. 5) for combat, Short Rests (6 hours) and Long Rests (8 hours) for recovery (see Rest, Basic Rules pg. 5), and full days for travel and downtime (see More on Downtime, Basic Rules pg. 5). Offtime counts exactly as much as active adventuring does. The GM adds all of it together as it happens, in days, and once that running total reaches 360, the character ages 1 year. Whether that year also brings a Growth depends on how many years the current wait requires, see the escalating schedule above.

Character Level

Character Level (Ch. Lvl) is where a build actually takes shape. Every Ch. Lvl, the player rolls a fresh Attribute Repository (see Attribute Development, Basic Rules pg. 5) to spend REM Points on Attribute Scores, Talents, Skills, and Traits (TST), and is granted a new Ability (Tactics/Spells) pick from their Class Tree, with a second pick at the midpoint toward the next Ch. Lvl (see Character Level XP Progression, below, for the exact midpoint XP math). Apex and Spirit need no separate spending at all, they climb automatically alongside Fortitude and Willpower (see Apex, Spirit, Basic Rules pg. 2). A new character reaches Ch. Lvl 1 almost immediately, often before the first conversation with an NPC wraps up, so the point-spending system is in the player's hands from the very start. Table talk will often shorten this to just "Level," but it always means Ch. Lvl unless Growth is specified.

At the table's preference, campaigns may use milestone leveling, where GMs announce XP based on story events that guide advancement.

Abilities within a Class Tree grow more powerful the deeper a character climbs, not evenly, each one further in is a meaningful jump over the last. A character has 59 Class Ability picks total across their entire career, to divvy across as many trees as they like, one pick at each Ch. Lvl and a second at the midpoint toward the next Ch. Lvl, all the way from Ch. Lvl 1 through 30. Only one Class Tree, ever, may reach its Ch. Lvl 30 Ability, the capstone, and the single most powerful one a character will ever wield; every other tree stops at its Ch. Lvl 29 Ability at most, one short of its own capstone, the same one-capstone-per-character rule Drawn Abilities use (see Drawn Abilities, above), just applied to the 30-Ability Class Tree and the 59-pick total instead of the 20-Ability Drawn tree and 40-pick total. The capstone itself is the one exception to the midpoint-pick pattern: it can only be claimed on a full Ch. Lvl, never at a midpoint, no matter how many picks a character has already banked toward that tree. A tree pushed hard from Ch. Lvl 1 reaches its 29th pick, one short of its capstone, by Ch. Lvl 15, well ahead of Ch. Lvl 30 itself; from there a player can claim that capstone on any later full Ch. Lvl right away, or hold off and pour both remaining Character Levels and midpoints into a second tree instead, only settling which tree gets its true 30th when a full Ch. Lvl finally comes due. Every weapon-specific Ability still requires its class's proper Armament readied to activate, same as Drawn Abilities (see Weapons Carried, Basic Rules pg. 4).

Example A: Maxed Capstone

A character dedicates their career to raw power: one tree, whichever the player chooses, pushed all the way to its Ch. A character deliberately chooses a middle-ground set instead of chasing a capstone: three trees built to a solid, balanced depth of 20 + 20 + 19 = 59. No tree reaches its 30th Ability, but all three carry real weight in play.

Example C: Maximum Diversity

A character goes as diverse as the system allows: 59 different trees, one Ability picked from each. No tree gets anywhere close to deep, but the character can call on a wider spread of Abilities than any other build in the game.

Character Level XP Progression

Ch. Lvl 1 through 10 use the same early-game pace as a familiar tiered XP reference table, unscaled, Ch. Lvl 1 costs nothing (a new character starts there immediately), and the next several levels stay cheap to keep the early game fast and empowering. From Ch. Lvl 11 onward, the cost of each level compounds roughly 20% over the last, so the climb steepens noticeably through the back half of the tree. Ch. Lvl 30 sits at roughly 2.58 million cumulative XP; it's not when a Class Tree's picks "run out" (a fully-dedicated tree already banks its 29th pick, one short of the capstone, by Ch. Lvl 15, see Character Level above), but it is the earliest a tree's Ch. Lvl 30 capstone pick becomes available, since a capstone can only be claimed on a full Ch. Lvl, never a midpoint (see When the Class Tree Runs Out, Basic Rules pg. 4).

XP Progression Examples

The specific award values below are illustrative, not a published baseline, see Module XP Award Guidelines below. They exist to show how award sizes need to scale alongside the XP Progression table above, not to set the numbers in stone.

Example A: Party Beginning a Ch. Lvl 1-5 Module Campaign

A party of Ch. Lvl 1 characters begins a Ch. Lvl 1-5 module. Over several sessions, they:

  • Defeat the module's main mini-boss encounter: 800 XP
  • Discover a hidden dungeon area: 400 XP
  • Complete three major quests: 1,200 XP each (3,600 XP total)
  • Survive a dangerous ambush encounter: 500 XP

Total: 5,300 XP accumulated. The party continues and completes a secondary objective (1,200 XP), reaching 6,500 XP total, exactly the threshold for Ch. Lvl 5.

Example B: Party Continuing into a Ch. Lvl 15-20 Module Campaign

A party that has reached Ch. Lvl 15 (161,300 cumulative XP) continues into a module built for that band. Higher-level modules need to award proportionally larger XP per achievement to keep pace with the steeper curve:

  • Defeat a major regional threat: 60,000 XP
  • Complete the module's central quest chain: 90,000 XP total across multiple quests
  • Discover a hidden lore vault: 30,000 XP
  • Survive a climactic siege encounter: 65,000 XP

Total: 245,000 XP gained, carrying the party from 161,300 to 406,300, just short of the 406,700 needed for Ch. Lvl 20, closed out by any smaller side objective. Compare the award sizes here to Example A: they're roughly 50-75 times larger, tracking the same growth the XP Progression table itself shows between these two bands.

Attribute Development and REM Point Spending

Attributes govern raw capability, how physically powerful, resilient, perceptive, charming, animated, and the like a Character is. Traits determine how Characters apply that capability under pressure.

Attributes are the innate capacities characters are born with that allow them to perform certain cognitive and physical tasks. Attributes begin at 0 by default, regardless of race and class, except where a race's Innate Trait Adjustments would otherwise put a Trait score above its aligned Attribute Score before play even begins. Because a TST score can never exceed its aligned Attribute Score, a race with a positive innate Trait bonus begins with that Trait's aligned Attribute raised to match it, see Starting Attribute Floor on each race's entry, Basic Rules pg. 2. Attributes serve many purposes, including as Prerequisites for gear, including Armaments and equipment, and to acquire talents, skills and Traits as well.

The seven Attributes; artistry, fortitude, intellect, perception, physique, resilience, and wisdom. Through their scores, purchased throughout progression, Attributes determine how strong a character is in a variety of areas, including; Abilities, talents, skills and Traits, referred to as Talents, Skills, and Traits (TST). Attribute Scores also serve as Prerequisites and more.

The Seven Attributes

Artistry (ART)

Creativity, performance, dexterity-based crafts, and deception.

Fortitude (FRT)

Physical resilience, endurance, and resistance to ongoing effects.

Intellect (INT)

Mental acuity, spellcasting power, reasoning, and willpower.

Perception (PER)

Awareness, detection, intuition, and luck.

Physique (PHY)

Physical strength, speed, agility, and toughness.

Resilience (RES)

Magical resistance and protection from elemental damage.

Wisdom (WIS)

Knowledge, intuition, and insight.

Attribute Development

To develop Attributes, starting at Level 0 (character creation) and again at every new Character Level (Ch. Lvl), players roll 1d6 9 times, discard the two lowest results, and assign the remaining 7 results as points to their respective slots in the Attribute Repository. At Level 0, all results are assigned and locked in, once finalized, the character advances to Ch. Lvl 1.

Each Attribute can be assigned a number of points between 1 and 6 in its respective Repository slot, and those REM Points are spent to purchase: the score of the Attribute (default 3 REM Points each), and/or talents, skills and Traits TST (default 2 REM Points each) aligned with that Attribute, OR saved as Remnants. The main purpose of Remnants is to hold left over REM Points that players weren't able to spend on Attributes and/or TST.

Before ending the progression process, anything left over is considered a REM Point and can be saved until the next level where the rolling and REM Point spending process is repeated. No REM Points need to be spent on Attribute Score; they can be saved as Remnants to be spent on items later in the game. Remnants allow players to save REM Points they could not spend and use them in future levels. Remnants may also be saved to spend on special items later in the game. Once a REM Point is spent to purchase an Attribute Score or TST, it cannot be refunded, it's locked in. There's no turning back.

Remnants: Items, like Relics and elements used to craft rare items, purchased with REM Points (Remnants) are generally expensive. As much as 180 REM Points can be saved by Ch. Lvl 30. This system has no stopping point, Ch. Lvl keeps granting fresh Attribute Repository rolls indefinitely. TST caps still apply once reached, but REM Points can always be collected and saved as Remnants. Apex and Spirit are never purchased with REM Points or any other currency, they rise automatically with Fortitude and Willpower; Life, Mana, Stamina, and Endurance grow automatically through Vitality Progression as well, not through direct point purchase.

Wellness

Wellness is a percentage, tracked per character, entirely separate from Apex, Spirit, and every Vitality resource, it never touches any of them, and it's not a Character Condition either (see Basic Rules pg. 5). It starts at 0%, and ranges from a -150% floor to a +150% ceiling. Sustained neglect ticks it down, sustained healthy living ticks it up, both automatically, both governed by the same schedule below, just running in opposite directions. There is no point cost anywhere in Wellness, a tick only ever moves the percentage itself.

Distance from 0%Days per Tick (base)
0% - 49%No tick
50% - 74%6
75% - 99%4
100% - 124%2
125% - 150%1

Each time a tick lands, Wellness moves 1% further in whatever direction it's already heading, and the day-count resets to zero and restarts at whatever rate matches the new percentage; crossing into a faster band on that tick means the very next one already runs at the new, faster rate. Within -49% to +49% nothing ticks at all, that's the neutral band. The Trait Health modulates this schedule: past -50% each point of Health's tier (floor(Health ÷ 5)) adds 1 day to the current band's tick length, slowing the decline; past +50% each tier instead removes 1 day, minimum 1, speeding the climb. See Trait Secondary Effect Formula, below, for how tiers work.

Example, Kaida's Vitality:

Her Stamina total of 554 (Base 32 + (38 x 12) + Willpower 16 = 504, x 1.10 for 10 Apex = 554.4, rounds to 554) and Endurance total of 476 (Base 35 + (38 x 11) = 453, x 1.05 for 5 Apex = 475.65, rounds to 476) work the same way. Her Mana (Base 32 + (38 x 12) + Wit 14 = 502) receives no Apex allocation, she isn't a caster.

Spirit = 16, equal to Kaida's Willpower score, split 10/Life, 6/Stamina, 0/Mana, regenerating 10 Life Chips and 6 Stamina Chips every Turn on top of whatever her Traits regenerate at Score 5+ (see Trait Secondary Effect Formula, below).

Vitality Recap: Apex always equals Fortitude's score. Spirit always equals Willpower's score. From there, a player chooses which Vitality resource(s) get the benefit: Apex can be allocated to any of the 4 (Life, Mana, Stamina, Endurance), Spirit can be allocated to any of the 3 (Life, Mana, Stamina, never Endurance).

Although there isn't a minimum (Attributes can be 0), characters may not apply more than 2 points of their roll results to any given Attribute at every level of progression. No Attribute may exceed a score of 50. For example, at level 2, an Attribute Score may be 0, but can be no higher than 4 before the process ends.

The Attribute cap of 50 is a theoretical maximum, achievable only by dedicating 2 points per level to one Attribute for 25 levels. Because each Attribute is individually capped at 50 and points are spread across all Attributes, most characters will have one high Attribute and the rest significantly lower, encouraging specialization. Players should expect most Attributes to fall between 12-25 unless they deliberately hyper-specialize.

It's important to note that TST scores cannot be above the score of their aligned Attribute. So if a character has a score of 1 in physique, the score of one of its aligned Traits Strength may not be greater than 1. This keeps Attribute Scores relatively low because they are often used as modifiers.

Players first need a score of 1 in any Attribute before purchasing any TST aligned to it because 1 is the †default Prerequisite for purchasing TST. The default price for any TST is 2 REM Points. Again a TST score cannot exceed its aligned Attribute Score, so with that score of 1, a character can go no higher than 1 for any TST aligned to it. There isn't a maximum of TST types though, so with enough REM Points (Remnants), a character could have any number of TST with a score of, in this case 1.

REM Point spending example: Say one of a player's rolls results in a 6, assigned to physique on the Repository. This gives them six REM Points to spend on physique and its TST scores. First the Attribute itself needs a score of 1, at a cost of 3 REM Points because it's the default score needed to purchase TST. This leaves 3 REM Points (of the original 6) in the Repository, but a balance can't be higher than 2, so the player needs to purchase a TST at 2 REM Points, which leaves 1 REM Point, lower than 2, so it can be saved until the next level.

Players have the freedom to allocate REM Points according to their preferences. These REM Points can enhance athletic or cognitive talents, attack and Protection Abilities such as evasion, Traits like strength and agility, allowing for a customized character build.

Note: At each level of progression, characters are charged a maintenance fee. This fee covers various aspects of upkeep, with costs varying depending on the specific type or variant of each item or service, such as armament repairs, ammunition, hygiene, and health supplies.

Rank

Rank is measured by Rank Points (RP) and reflects a hero's reputation, achievements and more. As a character gains RP through exceptional actions and exploration, Rank advances in this ascending order: Pauper, Peasant, Commoner, Adventurer, Hero, Uber, Elite, and Legend. Hostile Creatures and NPCs have Ranks too, and group challenges are balanced by averaging Ranks across the group, for instance a pauper matches to a Common NPC.

NPCs and MAC (Monsters, Animals, Creatures/Critters, see MAC, Basic Rules pg. 7) each carry a Rank that reflects their power and determines the challenge and rewards of an Encounter. Their ranks in ascending order are: Common, Uncommon, Rare, Unique, Heroic, Elite, Legendary, and Divine. Defeating foes of similar Rank awards standard Experience Points (XP), overcoming stronger opponents yields greater rewards, and trivial Encounters grant little or no XP. NPCs ranked Heroic and above carry fictional names and possess special Abilities and gear.

Character Rank advances as a character accumulates Rank Points through exceptional actions, exploration, and achievements, rising through: Pauper, Peasant, Commoner, Adventurer, Hero, Uber, Elite, and Legend.

Characters cannot use items that are of a rank above their own.

Rank is a measure of a character's overall reputation and power, determined by accumulating Rank Points (RP) from achievements, exceptional actions, and exploration. RP is awarded for significant accomplishments, and can be lost if reputation is diminished. Rank governs access to certain Abilities, quests, and items, and is used by the GM to determine the difficulty and rewards of Encounters, as well as to balance challenges between individual characters or groups.

Rank Level Prerequisites

Advancing through Rank tiers requires two Prerequisites, met independently of each other and of RP: a minimum Ch. Lvl, and a minimum Growth (Beginning Age + Years), a character's own chosen Beginning Age (see Age, Basic Rules pg. 2) plus a fixed number of years for that tier. The years-added side of that formula reuses the same numbers Growth's own escalating schedule is built on (see Growth Development, above), but this gate grants none of Growth's Vitality increases or Drawn Ability picks, those stay on Growth's own separate track regardless of Rank. Both gates work purely as thresholds: reaching them unlocks a rank, they do not grant it. Because a character's Beginning Age is itself chosen from a 5-year range at creation, the actual age required at every rank is a range too, not a single number, see the worked examples below the table.

Rank Requirement

Rank gates access on two fronts, challenges and usage.

Challenges: A character must be of the same Rank as a monster or challenge to engage it. Higher-ranked Monsters, Encounters, Lore Quests, and Bosses aren't secret, a party can see them, know they're there, but they function as obstacles, not accessible content, until Rank catches up. This does not lock a party out of the module they're actively playing, the module's own Rank-appropriate content remains accessible to characters working toward that module's Rank through it (see RP Earning from Modules below), since RP is earned by engaging that very content. The restriction bites hardest reaching ahead, into future modules, expansions, or Rank-locked Lore Quests and Bosses beyond what the current module offers.

How GMs Adjudicate "Hidden"

Content above a character's Rank isn't invisible, a party can see a higher-rank MAC prowling a ridge, or notice a fortified gate ahead. What they can't do is challenge it: it holds the line as an obstacle, not an Encounter they can accept, until Rank catches up. In practice, this looks like:

  • Physical barriers: road guards or checkpoint sentries turning the party back at a border, a warded gate that won't open, a collapsed bridge with no safe crossing.
  • Geographic barriers: a cliff face too steep to climb without Rank-appropriate gear or a high Olympiad score, a river with a current too strong to ford, terrain that simply routes the party elsewhere.
  • Visible but unengageable MAC: a higher-rank creature the party can observe from a distance but cannot approach, attack, or interact with as a standard Encounter, it holds its ground as scenery, not content.

The block only runs one direction. A higher-rank MAC or environment may still challenge the party, an aggressive, powerful MAC noticing and pursuing them, or an environmental hazard triggering regardless of Rank: a waterfall dragging characters over its edge, a landslide, a hurricane, an earthquake. When this happens, the party is at a serious disadvantage, they're facing content built for a Rank they haven't reached. Characters who get lost or wander into a higher-ranked area should feel genuinely endangered by it, not merely locked out of it.

Because Encounters, items, and Companions above a character's Rank are all gated the same way, none of them can be found through exploration either, see RP Earning from Modules and Rank and Armaments in Higher-Ranked Modules, below.

Usage: Characters must reach the rank of any item, armament, or equipment to equip or use it. Rank requirements apply to all items without exception. Acquiring or possessing an item does not grant Rank Points, items are restricted to characters who have already earned the corresponding rank through module-specific achievements, exploration, and exceptional actions. If a character legitimately ends up holding an item above their own Rank, gifted, traded, inherited, they may still carry, store, or gift it, provided they have sufficient Strength or storage capacity for it (see Encumbrance, Basic Rules pg. 5), but they cannot equip or activate it until their Rank matches. This is separate from exploration: an above-Rank item cannot be found by exploring blocked content in the first place (see above).

RP Earning from Modules

Each module records specific RP values for achievements, tasks, and discoveries based on their difficulty. Characters must meet both the Ch. Lvl and Growth (Beginning Age + Years) requirements for each rank before they can advance to that rank (see Rank Level Prerequisites above). Characters may enter and explore a module above their own Rank, but any monster, challenge, quest, Encounter, item, or Companion within it that exceeds their Rank is blocked off as an obstacle until they catch up, see Rank Requirement above. Only the module's Rank-appropriate content, matching a character's current Rank, is actually findable. For information on how characters earn Growth and Character Levels, refer to Growth Development and Character Level above.

GMs may simulate a character's Ch. Lvl and Growth (age), but characters MUST earn Rank, it can never be simulated, no exception. See Character Simulation, below, for the full procedure.

Rank and Death

Rank is only capped when Ch. Lvl is lost through the Death Penalty (see Basic Rules pg. 5), age is never lost to death, so the Growth (Beginning Age + Years) gate is unaffected. A character's Rank can never exceed what their current Ch. Lvl permits, per Rank Level Prerequisites above, so a Ch. Lvl lost to death may cap Rank at a lower tier until the Level is regained. Rank Points already earned are never lost or spent down; they stay banked in full, and Rank re-establishes automatically the moment the character's Ch. Lvl reaches that Rank's Prerequisite again.

Companions and Rank

Gaining a Companion does not grant RP. However, characters can earn RP by discovering Companions during exploration. Characters must be at the Rank of any Companion to have it follow them. Companions found in a module will not exceed that module's Rank. Lower-ranked Companions are not available in higher-ranked modules, as they are presumed to have been acquired in earlier, lower-ranked modules.

Rank and Armaments in Higher-Ranked Modules

Characters entering a higher-ranked module with a lower personal Rank can access and complete the portions of it that match their own Rank, same as anywhere else, higher-ranked Encounters, areas, and their loot stay blocked off as obstacles (see Rank Requirement, above) until Rank catches up. There's no way to get ahead on gear by wandering into content above a character's Rank; the only way in is earning it.

Rank Progression Examples

Example A: Party Beginning a Character Level 1-5 Module Campaign

A party of Ch. Lvl 1 characters (Pauper rank) begins a Ch. Lvl 1-5 module with Common-rank items. Over several sessions, they:

  • Defeat the module's main mini-boss encounter: 150 RP
  • Discover a hidden dungeon area: 75 RP
  • Complete three major quests: 100 RP each (300 RP total)
  • Discover a Companion during exploration: 100 RP

Total: 625 RP accumulated. The party continues and defeats a secondary objective (75 RP), reaching 700 RP total. Once they also reach Ch. Lvl 5, they advance to Peasant rank and can now equip Peasant-rank items found in later modules.

Example B: Simulated Character Level 6 Character Beginning a Character Level 5-8 Module Campaign

A GM simulates a Ch. Lvl 6 character to begin a Ch. Lvl 5-8 module campaign. The character is Ch. Lvl 6 but still Pauper rank because they haven't accumulated the 700 RP needed to reach Peasant rank yet.

The module's Peasant-rank (Uncommon) content is blocked off from this Pauper character as obstacles rather than accessible Encounters or loot:

  • A locked armory door the character can see but cannot open
  • A patrolling Uncommon-rank guard captain the party must avoid rather than engage
  • A fortified checkpoint turning them back toward the module's Common-rank areas

The character can still fully engage the module's Common-rank content and earn RP toward Peasant rank through it. Once they reach 700 RP (Ch. Lvl 5 is already met), the Peasant-rank obstacles above open up as genuine content, the armory door, the guard captain, the checkpoint, all newly accessible, with their gear and Companions findable for the first time.

Example C: Simulated Ch. Lvl 15 Character Skipping Ahead into a Higher-Ranked Module

A GM simulates a Ch. Lvl 15 character, and a matching Growth (Age) of +12, so a new player can jump straight into a Ch. Lvl 15-20 module without playing the earlier ones. Both simulated numbers clear Commoner's Level/Age gate (Ch. Lvl 9 / +4) comfortably, but the character is still Peasant rank, because RP is never simulated and this player hasn't earned any yet.

The module's Rare-rank content, matching its higher Character Level band, is blocked off from this Peasant character as obstacles:

  • A Rare-rank guardian construct blocking the only path forward, too powerful to engage
  • A sealed vault the party can see but cannot breach
  • A Rare-rank Griffon nesting on a cliff too high to safely reach without Commoner-rank climbing gear

The character can engage whatever Peasant-rank content the module offers and earn RP toward Commoner rank through it. Because this character skipped every earlier module, they never picked up the Common- and Uncommon-rank starting gear a Pauper or Peasant character normally accumulates along the way, they have nothing on hand appropriate to their actual earned Rank. The GM directs the player to the downloadable PDF version's back-catalog to acquire Peasant-rank-appropriate starting gear matching what those earlier modules would have provided, so the character isn't left without usable gear while working toward Commoner rank and the obstacles above.

Character Rank vs. Encounter Rank

Character ranks are named differently from armament and encounter ranks for roleplay purposes, a character's rank as a hero reflects their reputation and story, while the world uses consistent terms for threats and gear. They match directly: each character rank corresponds to the encounter and armament rank of the same tier and color.

Characters must match the rank of any armament or item to use it, plus any additional Prerequisites listed on the item itself (such as minimum Trait scores or class requirements).

Character Simulation

Modules are level-banded, and not every table starts at Ch. Lvl 1, a GM may need to simulate a higher Ch. Lvl (and matching Growth (Age)) character so a new player, or a late-joining party member, can start inside a module built for a higher band. Characters MUST earn Rank. It can never be simulated, under any circumstance. This is the one firm line in an otherwise flexible system, be clear about it at the table before simulating anyone.

What Gets Simulated

  • Character Level: Assign a starting Ch. Lvl matching the module's band or the GM's judgment call for where the party is headed.
  • Growth (Age): Assign a matching Growth (Age), so the character's simulated years of experience line up with their simulated Ch. Lvl rather than looking like a prodigy or an impostor. This can satisfy the Level/Age gate in Rank Level Prerequisites above.
  • Attribute Repository rolls: Run the Ch. Lvl 0 roll, then one roll per simulated Ch. Lvl, in order, exactly as if the character had played through them, see Attribute Development and REM Point Spending, above. There is no shortcut roll for "all levels at once"; each level's roll and spend happens individually so REM Point and Remnant totals stay honest. A GM running a one-shot or fast pickup game may instead average expected results (roughly 2-3 points landing in an Attribute's top slots per level) rather than rolling every level out, use judgment for how much table time simulation is worth versus getting back to the story.
  • TST and Ability picks: Spent and granted at the same points they'd have landed if actually played, TST following normal REM Point costs, Ability picks following normal Class Tree Slot order.
  • Vitality: Backfilled to match the simulated Growth (Age), since Vitality only ever comes from Growth (see Growth: Vitality, above), simulating a character's age this way is what lets their Life/Mana/Stamina/Endurance line up with the rest of the simulated build.

What Never Gets Simulated

  • Rank: Always the character's real, earned Rank (usually Pauper, unless they've genuinely earned RP elsewhere), regardless of how high their simulated Ch. Lvl or Growth (Age) go.
  • Rank Points (RP): Only ever the real total behind that real Rank.
  • Equippable Rank Tier: Set by the character's actual earned Rank, never by their simulated Ch. Lvl or Growth (Age). A simulated character cannot equip or use any item, Companion, or reward above that real Rank, see Rank and Armaments in Higher-Ranked Modules and RP Earning from Modules, above.
The Trade-Off of Simulating Age Forward: Retirement Age never moves, it's always the character's real Starting Age plus 40, fixed by race, no matter what Growth (Age) gets simulated. Simulating a character further into their Growth (Age) range doesn't buy them more time; it spends it. A character simulated to Growth (Age) +17 already has only 23 of their original 40 years left before Retirement, and still has to earn every bit of RP required for every Rank they reach in whatever real play remains. Simulating age can clear a Rank's Level/Age gate instantly, but it can never make Legend (Growth (Age) +30) easier to reach, push it far enough and it only brings Legend closer to being foreclosed by Retirement entirely.

Character Level Simulation Examples

Example A: Simulating a Ch. Lvl 5 Character for a Starter Module

A GM needs to backfill a player joining an existing Ch. Lvl 5 party. Growth (Age) is set to +2, matching Peasant's Level/Age gate, since the story has this character adventuring a couple of years already.

  • Run the Ch. Lvl 0 Attribute Repository roll, then one roll per simulated level, 6 rolls total.
  • Each roll's 7 kept results (1d6 x9, drop the lowest 2) typically total somewhere around 27-30 REM Points.
  • Across all 6 rolls, that's roughly 165-180 REM Points spent and saved across Attribute Scores, TST, and Remnants over the character's simulated life so far.
  • Rank stays Pauper. Nothing above touches RP, the character still needs to earn it in play.
Example B: Simulating a Ch. Lvl 20 Character for a Veteran One-Shot

A GM wants to run a single high-stakes session without a 20-level campaign behind it. Growth (Age) is set to +17, matching Uber's Level/Age gate, a genuinely veteran hero's history, not a prodigy's.

  • Run the Ch. Lvl 0 roll plus one roll per simulated level, 21 rolls total.
  • At the same rough 27-30 REM Points per roll, that's approximately 565-630 REM Points spent and saved across the character's build.
  • Even though Ch. Lvl 20 and Growth (Age) +17 together clear every Rank's Level/Age gate through Elite, the character remains Pauper rank unless the GM has separately awarded RP for this one-shot. Simulation never substitutes for earning it.

Starting Gear for Simulated Characters

A simulated character skipping straight into a higher-band module has never played the earlier modules that would have supplied Common- and Uncommon-rank starting gear appropriate to their actual, earned Rank. Direct the player to the downloadable PDF version's back-catalog to acquire Rank-appropriate starting gear matching what those earlier modules would have provided, see Example C under Rank Progression Examples, above, for a full worked case.

Since adventures are module-based, and each module includes Cards up to its own Rank tier (see Module Character Level to Card Rank, Basic Rules pg. 6), a simulated character reaches a new module already assumed to hold the Rank-appropriate gear of every prior expansion. Simulating Rank instead of tracking it honestly breaks this assumption.

The published table only runs through Growth 12 for reference, extending it further, and covering what (if anything) extended-play/houseruled campaigns do past Growth 8, still needs to be written here.

See Retirement tracking fields, Ch. I.

Not yet designed, decide whether these get paths at all, and if so, how many, what they're themed around, and how a player picks one.
